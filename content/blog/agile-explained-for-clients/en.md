## What Agile means from the client's side

**Agile** is an approach where a product is built not in one big phase — "spec, six months of silence, handover" — but in **short cycles**. At the end of each cycle you see a working part of the product, give feedback, and the team takes it into the next cycle.

For a client, this means three things:

- results arrive **earlier and in pieces**;
- the plan can **change along the way** without redoing everything;
- you need to be **involved regularly**, not just sign at the start and the end.

## How Agile differs from the classic approach

| | Classic (Waterfall) | Agile |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements | Fully fixed before start | Refined along the way |
| When you see results | At the end of the project | At the end of every cycle |
| Changes | Expensive, via contract amendments | A normal part of the process |
| Risk | Problems surface late | Deviations show up early |
| Client involvement | At the start and handover | Ongoing |

Neither approach is "always better". Waterfall works when requirements are truly known and stable. Agile wins when the product is new and much will only become clear after the first users.

## Key principles in practice

### Iterative delivery

Work is split into cycles, often one or two weeks long. The goal of each cycle is **a feature ready to show**, not "40% of the backend done". For example: first sign-up and catalog, then the cart, then payments.

### Priorities you can change

All tasks live in one list — the **backlog** — sorted by importance. You can reorder tasks or add new ones at any time. What changes is the next cycles, not the current one, so the team keeps its focus.

### Regular demos

At the end of a cycle the team shows what was done on a working version. This is your main checkpoint: not reports, but a live product.

### Feedback and improvement

After the demo you say what works and what does not. That goes straight into the backlog.

## What is expected from you

1. **Appoint a decision-maker** on the business side who can decide quickly. Without one, the team waits or guesses.
2. **Attend demos** and look at the product carefully.
3. **Set priorities**: what matters now and what can wait.
4. **Answer the team's questions** within a day or two, not weeks.
5. **Provide access** to people and data: users, the sales team, existing systems.

## How contracts and payments work

Agile does not combine well with fixing every feature and every deadline at once. Common options:

- **Time & Materials** — you pay for the time actually spent, with regular reporting.
- **Dedicated team** — you pay for a team for a period and steer the content through priorities.
- **Fixed budget, flexible scope** — the budget is known and the feature set adapts: the most important things first.

Whichever you pick, **transparency** matters: access to the task tracker, regular demos and clear reporting.

## Common client mistakes

- Expecting fixed scope, deadline and budget at the same time while changing requirements.
- Skipping demos and then being surprised by the result at the end.
- Changing priorities every day in the middle of a cycle.
- Handing the project to someone without authority to decide.
- Thinking "flexible" means "no planning". There is a plan; it just gets updated.

## FAQ

### Can you know the final cost upfront with Agile?

An exact figure is hard, because scope is refined along the way. But you can fix the budget and agree that the team builds the most valuable features first, so the budget yields a working product.

### How much time does a client spend on an Agile project?

Usually a demo at the end of each cycle, short calls about priorities and answers to questions. Regularity matters more than the number of hours.

### Are Agile and Scrum the same thing?

No. Agile is a set of principles, and Scrum is one specific framework that applies them through sprints, roles and meetings.
