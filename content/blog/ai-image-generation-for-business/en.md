## When image generation is genuinely useful

Image generators save time wherever you need **an illustration, not evidence**: blog covers, banner backgrounds, presentation concepts, moodboards, ad creative variants for testing. Where a picture is supposed to prove something — what the product looks like, who is on the team, what a client received — generation backfires. It stops being illustration and becomes misleading.

## How to choose a tool

Tools change fast, so compare them by criteria rather than rankings:

| Criterion | What to check |
|---|---|
| Quality for your task | Generate 10–20 images on your real tasks, not demo prompts |
| Commercial usage terms | Is it allowed on your plan, who owns the output |
| Style control | Style references, seeds, inpainting, partial edits |
| Text in images | How legible lettering is, especially non-Latin scripts |
| Privacy | Are uploads used for training, are generations public by default |
| Integration | Is there an API if generation must live inside your product |

Roughly, tools fall into **cloud services** (Midjourney, image generation in ChatGPT, Adobe Firefly and similar) and **open models** (the Stable Diffusion family, Flux) you can run on your own server. The former are easier; the latter give more control and keep data away from third parties.

## How to keep a consistent style

The main problem with brand imagery is that every picture looks like it came from a different universe. Discipline helps:

- **A prompt template.** Fix the constant part: style, palette, lighting, angle, aspect ratio. Change only the subject.
- **References.** Use style reference features or sample uploads where the tool supports them.
- **Brand palette.** Name specific HEX colors or describe the palette, then fine-tune color in an editor.
- **One tool per series.** Different models interpret the same words differently.
- **Final manual pass.** Cropping, color correction and typography belong in a regular editor, not the generator.

An example prompt structure:

```text
[subject], flat vector illustration, violet and lilac palette,
soft studio light, clean background, centered composition, 16:9
```

## Commercial usage rights

There is no universal answer, and two questions are easy to confuse:

1. **What the service allows.** Its terms of use decide whether you may use outputs commercially and on which plan. Read the current terms, not summaries.
2. **Whether the image is protected by copyright.** In some jurisdictions, including the US, images created without significant human creative input may not be protected. A competitor could, in theory, use a similar picture.

Practical rules:

- Do not generate **other companies' characters, logos, recognizable people** or imitations of a specific living artist.
- For a logo and core identity elements, hire a designer: they need to be registered and defended.
- Keep prompts and generation dates — useful if a dispute arises.
- For major campaigns, check with a lawyer in your country.

## When generated visuals hurt trust

- **"Team" and "client" photos** of people who do not exist.
- **Product images** that differ from the real product.
- **Artifacts**: extra fingers, melted text, odd geometry — audiences spot them quickly.
- **Generic aesthetics** people recognize as "AI art" and project onto the whole brand.

When in doubt, use generation for backgrounds and composition, and photograph real objects.

## A workflow for your team

1. Decide which tasks allow generation and which do not.
2. Approve prompt templates and a tool.
3. Generate a series of variants and pick the best.
4. Finish in an editor: color, crop, text.
5. Check for artifacts and resemblance to existing works.
6. Store source files, prompts and the license terms valid at generation time.

## FAQ

### Can I use AI images in advertising?

Usually yes, if your chosen service's terms allow it on your plan and the image does not infringe trademarks, characters or people's likeness.

### Is a generator suitable for creating a logo?

For exploring ideas, yes; for the final logo, better not. A logo must be unique, vector-based and registrable, which is a designer's job.

### How do I tell if an image looks too artificial?

Show it to people outside the team without context. If the first reaction is "that's AI", rework or replace it for key brand materials.
