## The short answer: what STAR is

Behavioral questions start with "Tell me about a time when...". The interviewer wants **a real example from your experience**, not an opinion. The STAR method helps you tell it in a structured way:

- **S, Situation:** the context in one or two sentences. Project, team, what was going on.
- **T, Task:** what you specifically were responsible for.
- **A, Action:** what you actually did. This is the core of the answer.
- **R, Result:** how it ended and what you took away from it.

A good answer takes about two minutes. **Situation and task: short. Actions: detailed. Result: clear.**

## How to prepare your stories

1. List five to seven stories from your experience: projects, internships, study teams, freelance work.
2. Mark S, T, A and R for each as bullet points, not a memorized script.
3. Check that one story can cover several questions: deadlines, ownership, a difficult colleague.
4. Say **"I"**, not only "we". The interviewer needs to hear your contribution.
5. If you lack work experience, use study projects, hackathons or open source. Scale matters less than honesty and lessons learned.

## Example answers

The examples below show the structure, not ready-made scripts. Use your own real stories.

### "Tell me about a conflict on your team"

- **S:** A designer and I disagreed: the mockup required a complex animation that lagged on low-end phones.
- **T:** I was responsible for building the screen and shipping it for the release.
- **A:** Instead of arguing in chat, I built two prototypes, the full version and a simplified one, and showed both on a real device. I suggested we agree on a criterion: smooth performance on the weakest target devices.
- **R:** We chose the simplified version that kept the key effect. Lesson: turn arguments about taste into arguments about testable criteria.

### "Tell me about a mistake you made"

- **S:** I shipped a database migration without testing it on a copy of production data.
- **T:** The change was mine, and so was the responsibility for the consequences.
- **A:** As soon as I saw errors, I told the team, rolled the change back using the prepared plan and wrote a short write-up of what happened and why.
- **R:** The service was restored, and the team added a mandatory migration check on a staging environment. Since then I always run migrations on a data copy first.

### "How did you handle a tight deadline?"

- **S:** A week before launch, the client added a new feature.
- **T:** We had to deliver it without hurting the quality of the main release.
- **A:** I split the feature into parts, estimated each one, and together with the manager proposed shipping a minimal version on time and the rest in the next release.
- **R:** The launch happened on schedule, and the extended version shipped later as agreed. Lesson: raise deadline risks immediately and bring options.

### "Tell me about a time you took ownership"

- **S:** The project build kept failing, and everyone worked around it manually.
- **T:** It was not formally my task, but it was slowing the whole team down.
- **A:** I agreed on time with the team lead, found the root cause and documented the build setup in the README.
- **R:** The manual workarounds were no longer needed. Lesson: small systemic problems are worth fixing rather than tolerating.

## Common mistakes

- **Speaking in generalities:** "I usually try to find common ground." You need a specific case.
- **A long backstory** and rushed actions.
- **No result or lesson.** Even when things went wrong, say what you learned.
- **Blaming others.** In conflict stories, show how you looked for a solution, not who was wrong.
- **Made-up stories.** Follow-up questions expose them quickly.

## FAQ

### What if I do not have a suitable story?

Say so honestly and use the closest example: from your studies, a personal project or volunteering. You can also describe what you would do, but a real example is always stronger.

### Is it okay to talk about a failure?

Yes, and it is often the best choice. Interviewers look at how you own mistakes and what you change afterwards. Make sure the story ends with a lesson and a change in how you work.

### Should I include numbers in the result?

If you have real data, yes: it makes the result concrete. If you do not, describe the result qualitatively and never invent figures, because follow-up questions will test them.
