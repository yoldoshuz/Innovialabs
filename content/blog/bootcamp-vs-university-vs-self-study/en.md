## The short answer

There is no universally best route. **University** fits if you have years to invest and need deep foundations or a degree. **A bootcamp** fits if you want a fast, structured entry and are ready to pay. **Self-study** fits if you can learn on your own and have a tight budget. Many people end up combining them: university plus personal projects, or self-study plus a short course with a mentor.

## Comparison on key criteria

| Criterion | University | Bootcamp | Self-study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost | Depends on the school and format, plus years without full income | Program fee, with a wide range | Minimal: plenty of free material |
| Time | Years | Months | As long as it takes — up to you |
| Depth | Math, algorithms, theory | Practice for a specific role | Uneven, depends on your plan |
| Employer perception | A degree passes formal filters | Projects matter, not the course name | Only portfolio and skills count |
| Discipline | External structure and deadlines | Schedule, mentor, cohort | Self-discipline only |

## University

**Pros:** systematic foundations that pay off on hard problems; a degree for visas and formal requirements; an environment with internships and connections.

**Cons:** slow; the curriculum may lag behind industry; often not enough hands-on practice.

**Best for:** recent school graduates, people aiming at research-heavy fields, and those planning to work abroad.

## Bootcamps and courses

**Pros:** an intensive program built around one role; mentors and code review; deadlines that keep you from quitting.

**Cons:** less depth than university; quality varies a lot; job guarantees can be marketing.

**Best for:** career changers who want external structure and feedback.

## Self-study

**Pros:** free or cheap; your own pace; the habit of figuring things out alone, which is key in IT.

**Cons:** easy to get lost in material; no feedback; high risk of giving up.

**Best for:** disciplined people with a clear goal who are ready to find a community and mentors themselves.

## How to vet a bootcamp before paying

Go through this checklist:

- **The full curriculum is published**, with topics and projects, not just "become a developer".
- **Who teaches:** mentors have current, verifiable industry experience.
- **Code review and feedback exist**, not just video lectures.
- **Graduate projects are visible** — real repositories, not screenshots.
- **Talk to graduates** directly instead of relying on testimonials on the site.
- **Any "job guarantee" is in the contract**: what exactly is guaranteed and under which conditions.
- **The refund policy** is clear before you pay.
- **A trial period or free intro module** is a good sign.

If a course pushes "discount ends today" and dodges specific questions, be cautious.

## Common mistakes

- Picking a route because of ads instead of your goal and resources.
- Assuming a course certificate alone will lead to an offer.
- Starting self-study with no plan and no way to measure progress.
- Dropping out of university for a bootcamp without considering whether you will need the degree later.

## FAQ

### Can I combine several routes?

Yes, and it is often the best option. Students add practice through courses and personal projects, while self-learners take a short course for code review and mentoring.

### How do I organize self-study?

Take requirements from real job postings, split them into topics, learn from official documentation and build a small project after each topic. Show your code to more experienced people regularly.

### Do employers prefer university graduates?

Some do, especially large companies and the public sector. Most, however, evaluate skills through a test task and interviews, where your learning route is secondary.
