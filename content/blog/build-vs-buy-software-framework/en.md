## The short answer

The key question is: **does this process set your business apart from competitors?** If not — accounting, email, a standard CRM — buy. If yes — unique pricing logic, your own customer experience, operational know-how — building often pays off. The remaining factors refine the decision: cost of ownership, timing, integrations and control.

## Five decision criteria

### 1. Differentiation

Split processes into two groups:

- **Commodity** — the same everywhere: bookkeeping, payroll, corporate email, support tickets.
- **Core** — the reason customers choose you.

Spending engineering effort on commodity means rebuilding something long solved. Bending your core around someone else's product means giving up your edge.

### 2. Total cost of ownership (TCO)

Compare more than license price against build price — look at everything over several years:

| Off-the-shelf | Custom build |
|---|---|
| Subscription or licenses, growing with users | Building the first version |
| Implementation and configuration | Hosting and infrastructure |
| Customization and integrations | Support, bug fixes, updates |
| Staff training | Team or contractor for further development |
| Migration if you switch vendors | Documentation and knowledge transfer |

A custom system is not "free after launch": support and evolution are an ongoing cost.

### 3. Time to market

Off-the-shelf software usually launches faster. If you need to test the market now, it makes sense to start with SaaS or no-code and postpone custom work until the process stabilizes and its limits become clear.

### 4. Integrations

List what the system must exchange data with: 1C, payment services, warehouse, website, messengers. For a ready product, check:

- whether there is an open API and webhooks;
- whether the local services you need are supported;
- whether data export is restricted.

When integrations are many and non-standard, the cost of gluing products together can match a custom build.

### 5. Long-term control

With a bought product you depend on the vendor: pricing, roadmap, data storage terms, a possible shutdown. With your own, you control everything — and are responsible for everything. Key questions: can you export all your data, where is it stored, what happens if the vendor leaves the market.

## The hybrid path

Often the best answer is "and", not "or":

- a ready core (CRM, ERP, payment gateway) plus your own modules on top via API;
- a ready platform now and gradual migration of core functions into your own code;
- an open-source solution adapted to your needs.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing only the first year's cost.
- Building because "the product doesn't fit 100%" without checking whether the business actually needs those differences.
- Buying from a demo without testing integrations on real data.
- Forgetting that a custom system needs an owner inside the company.

## Quick check

Answer yes or no:

1. Is the process unique and does it influence customer choice?
2. Do ready solutions require major changes to how you work?
3. Are integrations numerous and non-standard?
4. Do you have budget and people for support after launch?
5. Is full control over data critical?

More "yes" points toward building. More "no" points toward buying.

## FAQ

### Can I start with a ready product and move to custom later?

Yes, it is a common strategy. Make sure in advance that your data can be exported in an open format — that will make future migration easier.

### Is open source "buy" or "build"?

Somewhere in between. The license is free, but installation, customization, updates and security are on you, so calculate TCO as you would for a custom system.

### Who should make the decision?

Business and technical people together. The business defines what is core; technical specialists assess integrations, support and risks.
