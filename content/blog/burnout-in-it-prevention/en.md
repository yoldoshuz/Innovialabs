## What burnout is and how to spot it

**Burnout** is a state of exhaustion caused by chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. The WHO describes it through three dimensions: **exhaustion**, **increased mental distance and cynicism towards the job** and **reduced professional efficacy**.

Burnout develops gradually, and early on it is easy to mistake for ordinary tiredness. If the signs last for weeks and do not go away after a weekend, take it as a signal.

## Early signs in developers

- **Tiredness that does not pass** after rest and weekends.
- **Trouble starting tasks**: you open the IDE and cannot focus for a long time.
- **Cynicism**: "what does it matter, it will all be rewritten anyway", irritation in reviews and meetings.
- **Loss of interest** in what used to excite you: new technologies, your own ideas.
- **More mistakes** than usual and a sense of working on autopilot.
- **Work does not let go**: thinking about tasks in the evening, checking work chats before bed.
- **Physical signals**: sleep problems, frequent headaches.

These signs can also be linked to other conditions, so if you feel persistently worse, see a doctor or a psychologist.

## Common causes in IT teams

| Cause | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| Constant overtime | "Due yesterday" deadlines become the norm |
| Unclear priorities | Everything is urgent, plans change every week |
| No control | Decisions are made without you, but you are accountable for them |
| On-call and incidents | Frequent night pages without compensation or recovery time |
| No feedback | It is unclear whether your work is valued |
| Blurred boundaries when remote | The working day never ends |
| Monotony or context overload | Repetitive tasks, or five projects at once |

## Habits that help

- **A clear end to the working day**: a time after which you close work chats and the laptop.
- **Notifications under control**: turn them off outside working hours and use a do-not-disturb status.
- **Realistic planning**: estimate with a buffer and do not take on more than fits in a week.
- **Focus blocks**: time without meetings or messengers for complex work.
- **Real rest**: holidays without a laptop, weekends without "just a quick look".
- **The basics**: sleep, movement, breaks during the day. It sounds obvious, but these are the first things to suffer under overload.
- **Interests outside work**: activities that have nothing to do with screens and code.

## Conversations worth starting

Burnout is rarely solved alone, because many causes are organisational. Useful conversations with your manager:

- **About workload**: "I am on three projects right now and cannot do any of them well. Which one is the priority?"
- **About priorities**: ask for an explicit order when everything is "urgent".
- **About on-call**: discuss rotation and recovery time after night incidents.
- **About growth**: if the work has become monotonous, propose tasks or a role that bring growth back.

Talk about facts and consequences, not about how "everything is bad". A specific problem with a suggested solution turns into change far more easily.

## If you are a team lead

- Watch for signs in the team: withdrawal, irritability, a drop in quality from strong people.
- Do not reward heroics and overtime as the norm.
- Shield the team from constantly shifting priorities.
- Hold regular one-on-ones and ask about more than just tasks.

## FAQ

### How is burnout different from ordinary tiredness?

Ordinary tiredness goes away after rest. With burnout, rest helps only briefly, and exhaustion comes with cynicism about the job and a feeling that your efforts change nothing.

### Will changing jobs help?

Sometimes, if the cause is a specific environment. But if you carry over the same habits — no boundaries, agreeing to any workload — the situation can repeat. It is worth first understanding what exactly drains you.

### Should I tell my manager about burnout?

Usually yes: many of the causes are within their area of responsibility. Start with concrete facts about your workload and suggestions for what could change.
