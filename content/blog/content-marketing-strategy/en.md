## The short answer: what a strategy contains

A content strategy is not a monthly content calendar. It is the set of decisions the calendar follows from. A working strategy fits on one or two pages and answers:

1. **Who the audience is** and what jobs, fears and questions they have.
2. **What goal** content serves at each funnel stage.
3. **What topics and formats** you use.
4. **Where** the audience will meet your content.
5. **Who produces it** and how much.
6. **How you measure** results.

Here is each step in order.

## Step 1. Research the audience

Start with real customer questions, not post ideas. Sources:

- **Sales and support teams** — what people ask before and after buying.
- **Interviews with 5–10 customers** — why they chose you, what made them hesitate, where they looked for information.
- **Search suggestions and keyword tools** such as Google Keyword Planner — how people describe the problem in their own words.
- **Comments and reviews** on your channels and competitors'.

The output is 2–3 **segments**, each described by its job, purchase barrier, where they spend time and the language they use. More segments than that and the strategy gets diluted.

## Step 2. Set goals per funnel stage

One piece cannot attract strangers and close a deal at the same time. Split the jobs:

| Stage | Content goal | Example formats | What to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Attract new people with the problem | Answer articles, short videos, posts | Reach, organic traffic, new followers |
| Consideration | Show expertise and approach | Guides, breakdowns, comparisons, webinars | Time on page, subscriptions, return visits |
| Decision | Remove objections | Case studies, pricing and timeline FAQ, demos | Leads, page-to-lead conversion |
| Retention | Repeat sales and referrals | Newsletters, how-tos, product updates | Opens, repeat purchases |

Set a quarterly goal for the stage that matters most right now. Usually that is the weak point of the funnel: plenty of traffic but few leads means you should invest in decision-stage content.

## Step 3. Choose topics and formats

Put every audience question into a spreadsheet and group them into **topic clusters** — 3–5 broad themes where you have genuine expertise. For each theme:

- one **pillar article** or guide that fully answers the main question;
- several **supporting pieces** on narrow questions that link back to the pillar.

Pick formats by two criteria: how the audience prefers to consume information, and what the team can produce consistently. A solid article every two weeks beats daily posts that get abandoned after a month.

## Step 4. Plan distribution

Content without distribution barely works. Assign channels by role:

- **Owned**: website and blog, email list, Telegram channel. These are assets that compound.
- **Social**: Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube — adapt each piece to the platform instead of cross-posting it unchanged.
- **Paid**: promote your best pieces to warm audiences and use retargeting.
- **Partner**: guest posts, joint live sessions, industry communities.

Rule of thumb: one large piece gets cut into several units — a post, a carousel, a short video, a newsletter issue.

## Step 5. Assess resources

Write down honestly who does what: writer, editor, designer, subject expert, distribution owner. A common failure is asking the company's expert to "write in their spare time". A better setup is a 30-minute interview with the expert that a writer turns into an article.

Keep an **editorial calendar** with topic, funnel stage, owner, date and channels.

## Step 6. Define KPIs and a review cycle

Pick 1–2 metrics per stage from the table above. Tag links with UTM parameters and set up goals in your analytics so you see which pieces drive leads, not just views.

Review the report monthly and revisit the strategy quarterly: double down on topics and formats that deliver, drop what does not.

## Common mistakes

- Writing about the company instead of the customer's problems.
- Using a single metric (reach) for all content.
- Publishing without a distribution plan.
- Judging results after two weeks: organic content usually builds its effect over months.

## FAQ

### Where do I start with very limited resources?

Choose one segment, one owned channel and one format. Answer the ten most frequent customer questions — that alone produces material for sales and search.

### How often should we publish?

Consistency matters more than frequency. Pick a rhythm the team can sustain for at least six months and stick to it.

### How do I know content affects sales?

Tag links with UTM parameters, set up analytics goals and ask new customers where they heard about you. Look at assisted conversions, not just the last click.
