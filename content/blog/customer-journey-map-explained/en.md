## What a CJM is

A **customer journey map (CJM)** shows, step by step, how a specific type of customer moves toward a goal: from first realising a need to getting the result and coming back. It combines what the customer **does**, where they interact with the company, and how they **feel** at each moment.

The map is useful because no single team sees the whole journey. Marketing sees the ad, sales sees the call, developers see the app, support sees the complaint. A CJM puts all of it on one page from the customer's side.

## Key elements

| Element | What it describes | Example (ordering furniture online) |
|---|---|---|
| **Persona and goal** | Whose journey and why | A young family furnishing a new flat |
| **Stages** | Large phases of the journey | Search, compare, order, wait, delivery, use |
| **Actions** | What the customer does | Browses catalogue, measures the room, calls the store |
| **Touchpoints** | Where they interact with you | Website, Instagram, phone, courier, SMS |
| **Thoughts and questions** | What is on their mind | "Will it fit? When exactly will it arrive?" |
| **Emotions** | How they feel, often as a curve | Excited, then anxious while waiting |
| **Pain points** | What frustrates or blocks them | No exact delivery time, unclear assembly |
| **Opportunities** | Ideas for improvement | Delivery time slots, assembly video |

Stages should come from the customer's point of view ("compare options"), not from internal departments ("lead qualification").

## How to build a CJM step by step

1. **Choose one persona and one goal.** A map for "all customers" becomes vague. Start with your main persona and a concrete scenario.
2. **Set boundaries.** Decide where the journey starts and ends: from the first search to the first purchase, or including repeat orders and support.
3. **Collect data.** Interviews, support tickets, call recordings, reviews, analytics, and simply going through the journey yourself. Without data, the map shows the team's opinion.
4. **Define stages.** Usually several large phases are enough.
5. **Fill actions and touchpoints** for each stage, including offline ones such as calls and delivery.
6. **Add thoughts and emotions.** Use real quotes where possible. Draw the emotional curve: highs and lows show where attention is needed.
7. **Mark pain points.** Be specific: not "bad delivery", but "the customer does not know the delivery day and has to call twice".
8. **Find opportunities.** For each serious pain point, write one or more improvement ideas.
9. **Review with the team** across departments: everyone usually adds something the others did not know.

A whiteboard, a spreadsheet or a collaborative tool like FigJam or Miro works equally well. The format matters less than the content.

## From map to design improvements

A CJM that ends as a nice poster is wasted. Turn it into work:

- **Prioritise pain points** by how often they occur and how strongly they affect the goal. Low emotional points right before purchase or payment are usually the most costly.
- **Group fixes by type:** interface changes, text and communication, process changes, new features.
- **Write concrete tasks.** "Show available delivery slots on the order page" instead of "improve delivery".
- **Assign owners.** Many fixes cross teams: a delivery notification touches design, development and logistics.
- **Measure.** Choose an indicator for each improvement, such as fewer calls about order status or more completed checkouts, and compare before and after.
- **Update the map** after changes and new research.

## Common mistakes

- Building the map only from internal opinions.
- Mixing several personas and scenarios in one map.
- Describing the company's process instead of the customer's experience.
- Ignoring offline touchpoints such as calls, couriers and physical stores.
- Listing pain points without turning them into tasks.
- Creating the map once and never revisiting it.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a CJM and a user flow?

A user flow shows the steps and screens inside a product for one task. A CJM covers the wider journey, including channels outside the product, and adds the customer's thoughts and emotions.

### Do I need a CJM for a small product?

Even a simple one-page map helps if customers interact with you through several channels or if you do not understand why they drop off. For a narrow, single-screen task, a user flow may be enough.

### Should there be a "current" and a "future" map?

Often yes. A current-state map shows how things work today and where they break. A future-state map describes the desired experience and helps align the team on what to build next.
