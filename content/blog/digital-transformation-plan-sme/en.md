## The short answer

Digital transformation is not buying software. It is changing **how the company sells, serves customers and makes decisions** with the help of technology. A plan for an SME fits into five steps:

1. Assess the current state.
2. Set measurable goals.
3. Prioritize initiatives.
4. Budget in stages.
5. Manage the change.

A good plan fits on a few pages and is reviewed every quarter.

## Step 1. Assess the current state

Go through the main areas and honestly describe how things work today:

| Area | What to check |
|---|---|
| Sales and marketing | Where customers come from, where contacts are stored, whether leads get lost |
| Service | Contact channels, response speed, recurring questions |
| Operations | Inventory, orders, logistics, manual data transfer |
| Finance | Invoices, payments, reporting, time spent on reconciliation |
| Data | Whether key numbers for decisions are quick to get |
| People | Staff digital skills, readiness for change |

The result is a list of **pain points**: where money, time or customers are lost.

## Step 2. Goals

Phrase goals in business terms, not technology terms. Not "implement a CRM", but "stop losing leads and reply to customers faster". A good goal:

- is tied to revenue, costs, service quality or risk;
- has a metric you can measure now and in six months;
- has an owner — a specific manager.

Limit yourself to 2-3 goals per year. More than that and focus fades.

## Step 3. Prioritize initiatives

For each goal, list possible initiatives: CRM, online payments, a Telegram bot, integration with the accounting system, an analytics dashboard, an online store. Then rate each one by:

- **value** toward the goal;
- **effort** and complexity;
- **dependencies** — what must be done first;
- **risks** — what could go wrong.

Do the high-value, moderate-effort initiatives first. Mind the sequence: analytics is useless while data is scattered across spreadsheets, so data gets cleaned up first.

## Step 4. Budget

A transformation budget has several parts:

- licenses and subscriptions for ready-made services;
- development and integrations for your processes;
- data migration;
- staff training;
- support and improvements after launch;
- the time of your own people involved in the project.

Fund it **in stages**: pay for the first stage, review the result, then decide on the next. Keep a reserve for unplanned changes — they almost always come up.

## Step 5. Change management

Most transformation difficulties are human, not technical. What helps:

- **Explain why.** People need to understand what changes for them personally.
- **Appoint champions.** In each department, one person learns the system first and helps the rest.
- **Pilot.** Launch with one team, gather feedback, refine.
- **Training.** Short guides and videos instead of long manuals.
- **Retire the old way.** After the switch, close the old process, or both will keep running in parallel.

## How to write the plan down

One table is enough: initiative, goal, metric, owner, stage deadline, stage budget, status. Review it every quarter: what worked, what did not, what changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with technology.** Choosing a system before understanding the problem.
- **Doing everything at once.** The team cannot absorb the changes.
- **No measurement.** Without metrics you cannot tell whether there is any effect.
- **Delegating everything to a contractor.** An external team can build the system, but processes and decisions remain yours.

## FAQ

### Where should we start on a limited budget?

With the one pain point that hurts revenue or customers the most. Often that is lead tracking or online payments: the effect shows quickly and helps justify the next steps.

### How long does digital transformation take?

It is not a one-off project but an ongoing process. Individual stages can be planned for a few months, and the plan itself should be reviewed every quarter.

### Do we need an in-house IT specialist?

Not necessarily at the start. A business-side process owner matters more; the technical work can go to an external team, but priority decisions are best made in-house.
