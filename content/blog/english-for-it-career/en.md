## The short answer

**Yes, English matters**, but the requirements grow step by step. At the start, you need to read documentation and error messages confidently. Fluent speaking becomes necessary when you work with colleagues or clients from other countries.

Do not wait for "perfect" English before learning IT. Learn the language alongside it, tied to real tasks.

This guide is written for people whose first language is not English, so the stages describe a non-native speaker's path.

## What you need at each stage

| Stage | What you do | What you need |
|---|---|---|
| Learning | Docs, tutorials, error messages | Read technical text, understand terms |
| Local company | Code, tickets, commits, searching for solutions | Read and write short technical texts |
| International team | Calls, chat, reviews | Understand speech, explain ideas clearly |
| Interviews abroad | Talk about experience, solve problems aloud | Discuss technical decisions fluently |

### Reading documentation

This is the minimum for every role. Official docs, forum answers, library descriptions and error texts are almost always in English. The good news: technical English is repetitive. The vocabulary and patterns are limited, and you get used to them quickly.

### Working at a local company

You speak your native language, but the code is still in English: variable names, comments, commit messages, tickets. You need to write clearly and avoid mistakes that change the meaning.

### International team

Now **speaking** is added: daily stand-ups, task discussions, demos. Fluency matters more than perfect grammar. The valued skill is saying briefly and clearly what is done, what is blocking you and what comes next.

### Interviews

This is the most demanding stage: you describe your experience, think aloud while solving a task and ask clarifying questions. Prepare for it separately.

## A plan to improve technical English

1. **Switch your environment.** Set your IDE, OS and browser to English. The terms stick on their own.
2. **Read docs in the original.** Do not translate everything — keep a personal glossary of new words.
3. **Write in English every day.** Commits, comments, READMEs for your projects.
4. **Listen to technical content.** Conference talks, videos on your stack. First with subtitles, then without.
5. **Practice speaking in work scenarios.** Presenting a project, explaining a decision, giving a status update. Record yourself and listen back.
6. **Do a mock interview.** With a mentor, a colleague or a language club.

A short status update worth practicing until it is automatic:

```text
Yesterday I finished the login form validation.
Today I'm working on the password reset flow.
No blockers so far.
```

## Common mistakes

- **Postponing IT until your English is "good".** Language grows best on real tasks.
- **Learning general English unrelated to work.** Travel vocabulary will not help in code review.
- **Being afraid to speak because of an accent.** Accents are normal in international teams; clarity is what counts.
- **Machine-translating all documentation.** It distorts terms, and you do not learn.

## FAQ

### Can I get an IT job without English?

Some positions at local companies, yes — especially if your team and clients speak your language. But without reading documentation, growing will be noticeably harder.

### What level do international companies expect?

Enough to understand colleagues in conversation, explain technical decisions freely and handle written communication. Check the specific requirements in each job posting.

### Do language courses help?

Yes, if they include speaking practice. For the technical side, add reading documentation and writing in English in your daily work.
