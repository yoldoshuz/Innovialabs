## What going paperless actually means

It is not "scan everything". It is rebuilding a document's journey: it is **created digitally, approved in a system, signed electronically and stored in a digital archive**. Paper stays only where the law or a partner still requires it.

Four building blocks:

- **Electronic documents** — templates, one repository, versions.
- **Electronic signatures** — legally meaningful confirmation.
- **Approvals** — routes, deadlines, owners.
- **Archive** — search, access rights, retention periods.

## Electronic documents

Start by putting files in order:

- shared **templates** for contracts, acts, internal orders;
- one **repository** instead of personal folders and email attachments;
- clear **naming** and versioning rules;
- **metadata**: counterparty, date, amount, status — this is what people search by later.

## Electronic signatures

Signature types and their legal weight depend on your country's legislation. In practice, separate:

- **simple confirmation** in a system (login, button click) — fine for internal approvals;
- **qualified signatures** with a certificate — needed for documents with external parties and government bodies.

Before dropping paper with counterparties, check with a lawyer which documents can be signed electronically and how to agree on this with partners.

## Approvals

An approval route is a chain: who reviews, in what order, by what deadline. A good system:

- sends the document to the next participant automatically;
- reminds people about overdue tasks;
- keeps a history of who approved what, when and with which comments;
- lets people approve from a phone.

Do not copy paper bureaucracy into the system one to one. Digitization is a good reason to remove unnecessary sign-offs.

## Archive

A digital archive must answer three questions: **where the document is, who can access it and how long to keep it**. Set up access roles, backups and retention periods by document type. Run scanned documents through OCR so full-text search works.

## A phased migration plan

| Stage | What to do | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | List documents, their path, participants, legal requirements | Clear view of what to move and in which order |
| 2. Internal documents | Orders, requests, memos in the system | Staff get used to the new process |
| 3. Approvals | Routes for contracts and invoices | Deadlines and bottlenecks become visible |
| 4. Signing and exchange | E-signatures, exchange with counterparties | Fewer couriers and printouts |
| 5. Archive | Digitize needed old documents, set retention rules | Fast search, less storage space |
| 6. Integrations | Connect to CRM, inventory, accounting | Data is not entered twice |

Assign an owner and run training at every stage. Pilot with one department first, then expand.

## Common pitfalls

- **Digitizing everything at once.** A big-bang switch overloads people and breaks processes.
- **Ignoring the legal side.** An electronic document without the right signature may not be valid.
- **Scanning without structure.** Thousands of PDFs without metadata are just a paper archive on a disk.
- **Not planning access.** HR and financial documents should not be visible to everyone.
- **Keeping a parallel paper process.** If both ways are allowed, people choose the familiar one.

## FAQ

### Do we need to scan the entire old archive?

No. Digitize what people access regularly and what current work requires. The rest can stay on paper until its retention period ends.

### Which document management system should we choose?

Start from your processes: the document types you need, support for the e-signature accepted in your country, integrations with accounting and CRM, and mobile access. If off-the-shelf products do not fit, a system can be customized or built for you.

### How do we get employees to adopt the new system?

Show them it makes their work easier: less running around with paper and a clear status for every document. Training, short guides and gradually retiring the paper route all help.
