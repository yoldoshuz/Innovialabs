## The short answer

SEO pricing is driven by **scope of work**, not by some fixed "cost of promotion". The same agency can quote very different amounts for two sites, and that is normal. What matters more is **what you are paying for** and **how results are measured**.

## Pricing models

| Model | How it works | Pros | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monthly retainer** | Fixed monthly fee for a work plan | Predictable budget, whole-site work | Without reports, easy to pay for nothing |
| **Per position** | Pay for keywords ranking in the top | Feels simple | Agency picks easy keywords with no demand; rankings are not sales |
| **Per traffic** | Pay per visitor from search | Tied to a real outcome | Untargeted traffic can be inflated |
| **Per lead** | Pay per inquiry or call | Closest to business value | Hard to separate SEO from other channels |
| **Project** | One-off: audit, keyword research, migration | Clear deliverable and timeline | No long-term support |

In practice, models are often combined: a **one-off audit** to start, then a **retainer** with reporting on agreed metrics.

## What affects the cost

- **Niche competition.** Ranking a dental clinic in a capital city is harder than ranking niche B2B equipment: more strong competitors and more ad money.
- **Region and languages.** One city, a whole country or several; Russian, Uzbek, English — each version needs its own keywords and content.
- **Site size.** A landing page and a store with thousands of products are very different technical workloads.
- **Technical condition.** Slow pages, duplicates, indexing errors, no mobile version — these must be fixed before content can work.
- **Content.** Copy, articles, category descriptions — often the most expensive part.
- **Who implements changes.** If the agency has no code access or your developer is busy, work drags on.
- **Links and mentions.** A separate cost line in competitive niches.

## What a reasonable work plan includes

1. Technical audit and fixes.
2. Keyword research and mapping queries to pages.
3. Optimizing structure, headings and meta tags.
4. Creating and improving content.
5. Local SEO: map and directory listings for offline businesses.
6. Analytics: goals, traffic and lead reports.

If an offer contains none of these, ask what exactly will be done each month.

## Red flags in offers

- **Guaranteed number one or a specific position.** Search engines do not sell rankings, and nobody controls the algorithm.
- **Results "in a week".** SEO is a long channel; first changes usually show up after months.
- **No access to reports and analytics.** You should see Google Search Console and Yandex Webmaster data yourself.
- **Bulk link buying.** It can lead to penalties.
- **A "secret method".** A good contractor explains what they do and why.
- **Paying for rankings on keywords nobody searches for.**

## How to choose a format

- New site, or no idea what is wrong with it — start with an **audit**.
- Stable business with a budget for months ahead — a **retainer** with a clear plan.
- Need to validate demand quickly — run paid search in parallel and build SEO as a long-term asset.

## FAQ

### Why do agency quotes differ so much?

They include different scopes: one only edits meta tags, another handles technical optimization, content and analytics. Compare work lists, not totals.

### How long until results?

It depends on competition, site age and starting condition. Usually months, not weeks. Early signals — growing impressions and indexed pages — appear before traffic.

### Can I do SEO myself?

Yes, the basics — technical hygiene, meta tags, useful content — are within reach of an in-house team. A contractor helps when time or expertise in a competitive niche is lacking.
