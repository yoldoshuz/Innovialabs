## Where to actually start

Automation does not start with buying a CRM. It starts with one question: **what do your people spend time on every day, repeating the same actions?** The order is:

1. Describe the key processes.
2. Find the repetitive manual work in them.
3. Pick quick wins — simple, frequent tasks.
4. Measure time before and after.
5. Scale what worked.

Automating chaos is pointless: you get the same chaos, only faster.

## Step 1. Map your processes

Pick 3-5 processes that bring in money or take the most time. Typical ones for a small business:

- receiving and handling inquiries;
- invoicing and tracking payments;
- inventory and stock control;
- client bookings and reminders;
- reports for the owner.

For each process, write the steps as a simple list: who does it, what they do, in which tool, how long it takes. The best source is the people doing the work — ask them and watch how they do it. A spreadsheet is enough; complex notations are not needed.

## Step 2. Find the manual routine

Mark the steps that show signs of routine:

- **Moving data** between systems: from a messenger to a spreadsheet, from a spreadsheet to accounting.
- **Copying templates**: identical emails, contracts, invoices.
- **Manual reminders**: call the client, check the payment.
- **Assembling reports** from several sources.
- **Answering the same questions** from customers.

## Step 3. Pick quick wins

Rate each task on three criteria:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Frequency | How many times a day or week does it happen? |
| Time | How many minutes does one run take? |
| Complexity | Can a ready-made tool handle it without custom development? |

Start with tasks that are **frequent, uniform and simple**. Good candidates:

- website inquiries land in a CRM or Telegram chat automatically;
- invoices and contracts are generated from templates;
- clients get an automatic booking reminder;
- online payments are marked in your records without manual entry;
- a chatbot handles common questions.

Leave complex processes with many exceptions and approvals for later.

## Step 4. Measure the result

Without measurement you cannot tell whether automation paid off. A simple method:

1. For one week before the change, record how often the task runs and how long it takes.
2. Repeat the measurement after the rollout.
3. Multiply the difference by the frequency — that is hours saved per month.

Look beyond time to **errors**: lost leads, wrong amounts, forgotten calls. Sometimes their disappearance is the main benefit.

## Step 5. Scale up

Once the first improvements work, connect your systems: CRM, accounting, payments and website exchange data through integrations. Custom development may be needed at this stage, but the decision is now based on data rather than guesses.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with a big system.** Rolling out a complex ERP without clear processes often ends with staff going back to spreadsheets.
- **Not involving employees.** If a tool is inconvenient, people will work around it.
- **Automating rare tasks.** Something done once a quarter is not worth the effort.
- **No owner.** Every automation needs a person who checks that it keeps working.

## FAQ

### Do I need a developer to start automating?

Often not: many quick wins are covered by ready-made CRMs, online payment services and no-code tools. Development is needed when off-the-shelf tools do not connect to each other or do not fit your process.

### How much does small business automation cost?

It depends on the number of processes, available tools, required integrations and the amount of customization. That is why it makes sense to start with one quick win and assess the effect before investing in a large project.

### How do I know a process is ready for automation?

It should run regularly, the same way, by clear rules. If every employee does it differently, agree on one standard procedure first.
