## What scope creep is and why it hurts

**Scope creep** is when features and tweaks that were never in the agreed plan gradually enter the project, while the deadline and budget stay the same. Each request looks small: "just one more filter", "let notifications go to Telegram too". Together they eat weeks.

The problem is not change itself — a product should evolve as you learn. The problem is accepting changes **without an estimate and without a decision** on how to pay for them: with money, time or by dropping another feature.

## Why it happens

- **A vague specification.** If the document says "a convenient user dashboard", everyone reads "convenient" differently.
- **No fixed baseline.** There is nothing to compare a new request against, so nobody knows whether it is new work or part of the old.
- **Too many voices.** The CEO, marketing and sales all send wishes straight to developers.
- **The contractor avoids saying "this is out of scope".** They agree, then miss the deadline.
- **Ideas appear once people see the product.** That is normal, but those ideas need a process.

## How to keep scope under control

### 1. Fix the baseline

Before development starts, agree on a **baseline**: the feature list, acceptance criteria and what is explicitly **out of scope**. The "out of scope" section is often more valuable than the main list — it settles arguments in advance.

### 2. Introduce change requests

Every new request becomes a **change request** — a short record:

- what should change and why;
- who asked for it;
- the contractor's estimate: hours, impact on the deadline, risks;
- the decision: accept, postpone or reject.

This is not bureaucracy. It shows the price of a decision before work begins.

### 3. Appoint one product owner

The client side needs **one person** who makes scope decisions. Everyone else passes wishes through them, so the contractor never gets conflicting tasks.

### 4. Prioritize instead of only adding

When an important new feature appears, ask: **what do we remove or move to later in exchange?** The simple MoSCoW scheme helps:

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Must have | Launch is impossible without it |
| Should have | Important, but can ship a bit later |
| Could have | Nice if resources allow |
| Won't have (now) | Consciously postponed |

### 5. Keep a backlog for future versions

Good ideas do not need to be rejected — they need to be **written into the backlog** for the next iteration. People accept postponing an idea more easily when they see it is not lost.

### 6. Compare plan and reality regularly

A short weekly call with a demo of the work helps you notice early when things drift. The earlier a deviation is visible, the cheaper it is to fix.

## Common mistakes

- **Verbal changes in a messenger** with no record and no estimate.
- **A fixed price without a detailed scope** — disputes are inevitable because the sides understand differently what the price covers.
- **Banning change entirely.** A hard freeze produces a product that is outdated at launch. The goal is to manage change, not forbid it.
- **Decisions without a product owner.** The contractor has to guess whose opinion matters more.

## How to choose a working model

If the product is still finding its shape, **Time & Materials** with short iterations is more comfortable: change is built into the process. If requirements are stable and well described, a **fixed price** works, but then a change request process is mandatory.

## FAQ

### Can changes in a project be avoided completely?

No, and they should not be. Change is a normal part of development. The goal is for every change to get an estimate and a conscious decision instead of slipping into the work unnoticed.

### Who should estimate a change request?

The development team gives the estimate, and the client's product owner makes the decision. That way the technical cost and the business value are weighed together.

### What if the scope has already grown?

Pause and rebuild the task list: mark what was in the original plan and what was added later. Then set priorities together with the contractor and renegotiate the deadline or budget.
