## Where to start

Your first hire should not be the cheapest developer but a **tech lead** — someone who can write code, make architectural decisions and evaluate candidates. Without one, you risk hiring people nobody can guide and ending up with code that has to be rewritten later.

Before hiring, answer three questions:

- **What exactly will the team do** in the next 6-12 months: a new product, maintaining an existing one, integrations?
- **Which stack** fits those tasks — best chosen together with the tech lead.
- **Are you ready to manage** a team as an ongoing cost, not a one-off project?

## Hiring order

A typical sequence for a small product:

1. **Tech lead / senior full-stack** — architecture, stack, early decisions, help with hiring.
2. **One or two developers** for the main workload (backend, frontend or mobile, depending on the product).
3. **QA** — once releases become frequent and developers testing by hand starts slowing things down.
4. **Designer** — full-time or part-time, if the interface matters for the product.
5. **Product manager** — when there are more tasks than you as the owner can prioritize.
6. **DevOps** — often the tech lead or an external specialist covers this role at first.

Do not hire everyone at once. Add each next person when you see a specific bottleneck.

## Small team structure

| Size | Composition | Who leads |
|---|---|---|
| 2-3 people | tech lead + developers | tech lead and owner |
| 4-7 people | + QA, designer | tech lead + product manager |
| 8+ people | several product groups | engineering manager, group leads |

While the team is small, extra hierarchy only gets in the way. What matters more is that every task has a clear owner.

## Salaries as a budget line

Salary is only part of the cost. Plan for the **full cost of an employee**:

- salary and taxes;
- hiring: interview time, recruiter fees;
- hardware, licenses, cloud services;
- office or remote work allowances;
- training and conferences;
- onboarding time, when a newcomer is not yet fully productive.

Budget at least a year ahead: a team assembled for three months cannot be disbanded and rebuilt quickly without losses.

## Onboarding

Good onboarding shortens the time to the first useful task. The minimum set:

- **documentation**: how to run the project locally, architecture, coding conventions;
- **access** ready on day one: repository, task tracker, chats, environments;
- **a first task** that is small and real, reviewed by an experienced colleague;
- **a mentor** for the first weeks;
- a check-in after a month: what is unclear, what gets in the way.

## Processes from day one

Even a team of three needs basic rules:

- **Git flow and code review** — no code reaches main without review.
- **A task tracker** and a clear, prioritized backlog.
- **Short regular meetings**: planning, syncs, retrospectives.
- **CI/CD** — automated tests and deployment so releases do not depend on one person.
- **Documented decisions** — why a particular approach was chosen.

## Common mistakes

- Hiring only junior developers to save money — without a mentor they work slowly and pile up technical debt.
- Expecting results in the very first month.
- Not giving the tech lead a say in hiring.
- Keeping all knowledge in one person's head.
- Rejecting outside help entirely: at the start, a hybrid model (own team + contractor) often reduces risk.

## FAQ

### When is an in-house team better than a contractor?

When development is a permanent part of the business, the product evolves for years and you want to build expertise inside the company. For one-off projects and a quick start, a contractor is usually simpler.

### How long does it take to build a team?

It depends on the market and requirements: finding one experienced specialist can take anywhere from a few weeks to a few months. That is why you should start looking for a tech lead early.

### Can I start without a tech lead?

You can, if an experienced external specialist or contractor handles architecture and code review at the start. But without someone responsible for technical decisions, a team quickly loses direction.
