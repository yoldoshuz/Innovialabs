## The short answer

Good hosting is hosting you never think about. To get there, check the provider **before** paying: server location, real resources, availability history, support, backups, room to grow and contract terms. Below is a checklist you can go through in one evening.

## 1. Data center location

- **Closeness to your audience.** The closer servers are to users, the lower the latency. It is noticeable on dynamic pages and APIs.
- **Data requirements.** If you store personal data of citizens, check where the law requires it to be kept and pick a provider with a data center in that country.
- **Choice of region.** It helps if you can place a backup or a second server in another location.

## 2. Resources without fine print

- Are **specific** CPU, RAM, disk size and type listed, rather than a vague "up to" or "unlimited"?
- What kind of disk: NVMe, SSD or HDD?
- Are there limits on traffic, process count or CPU usage on shared hosting?
- Dedicated or shared cores? On shared cores, performance depends on your neighbors.

An "unlimited" plan almost always has limits in its acceptable use policy. Find them before you buy.

## 3. Availability history

The uptime figure in ads is a promise, not a fact. Check:
- whether the provider has a **public status page** with incident history;
- how it communicated past outages and how quickly it fixed them;
- what the SLA promises when it is breached: credits, refunds or nothing.

Read reviews with care: unhappy customers write more often, but repeated complaints about the same thing are an important signal.

## 4. Support

Before buying, send support a specific technical question. Evaluate:
- how quickly the answer came and at what time of day;
- whether it was a real answer or a template;
- which channels are available: tickets, chat, phone;
- which languages they support.

## 5. Backups

- Does the provider make backups, and is it included in the price?
- How often, how many copies are kept, where are they stored?
- Can you restore data **yourself**, or do you have to contact support?
- Can you download a copy?

Even if the provider makes backups, keep your own copy somewhere else.

## 6. Scaling

- Can you add resources without migrating or long downtime?
- Is there a growth path: shared hosting to VPS to dedicated server or cloud?
- Are load balancers, object storage and managed databases available if you need them?

## 7. Contract and terms

- Refund policy and minimum billing period.
- How to terminate and **how to get your data out**.
- Suspension rules: what can get a server switched off and whether you get a warning.
- Renewal price: the promotional first-year price is often much lower than the regular one.
- For companies: a formal contract and proper invoices.

## Red flags in cheap offers

- A price far below market with no clear explanation.
- "Unlimited" everything and no acceptable use policy.
- No company details, address or contract.
- No status page or incident history.
- Email-only support that replies in several days.
- You cannot export your data, or it is a paid extra.
- The domain is registered to the provider instead of you.

## How to test a provider in practice

Take the cheapest plan or a trial and run a short test: deploy your project, check response times, make and restore a backup, ask support a question. A few days of this will tell you more than any review.

## FAQ

### Should I pick foreign hosting because it is cheaper?

You can, if your audience and data allow it. Consider latency for local users, personal data storage requirements and payment options. Projects with a local audience and personal data more often choose a provider inside the country.

### Why is a status page better than a promised uptime figure?

The SLA describes a target, while a status page shows what actually happened: how many incidents there were, how long they lasted and how the provider communicated about them.

### Should I register my domain with the same provider?

Not necessarily, and a separate registrar is sometimes more convenient. What matters is that you or your company are listed as the domain owner in the registry and that you have access to manage it.
