## The short answer

A good vendor has already solved similar problems, shows you exactly who will work on your project, explains the process in plain words and puts everything in the contract. Price matters, but comparing it only makes sense between companies that passed the other checks.

## Step 1. Define what you need

Before contacting companies, prepare one or two pages:

- the **goal** of the product and who its users are;
- key scenarios (what a user must be able to do);
- desired launch date and budget constraints;
- what already exists: design, website, CRM, integrations.

Sending the same brief to every candidate lets you compare answers, not impressions.

## Step 2. Build a shortlist

Pick a few companies with projects in your niche or of a similar type: mobile app, online store, internal system, Telegram bot. Experience with a related problem matters more than a big name.

## Step 3. Check against the criteria

| Criterion | What to check | Good sign |
|---|---|---|
| Relevant experience | Similar projects you can open and try | They talk about problems they solved, not just results |
| Team | Who the manager, developers and designer will be | They introduce people before kickoff |
| Process | Stages, demo frequency, testing | They regularly show a working version |
| Communication | Speed and clarity of replies | They ask clarifying questions about your brief |
| Contract | Code ownership, payments, warranty | Code and access are transferred to the client |
| References | Contacts of past clients | They let you talk to a client directly |

## Step 4. Compare proposals correctly

Estimates from different companies often differ a lot. The reason is usually not greed but a different understanding of scope. Ask each one:

- what is included and what is not (design, testing, publishing, support);
- what assumptions were made;
- how work will be estimated if requirements change.

The lowest price with no breakdown is a reason for caution, not celebration.

## Step 5. Start small

If you are unsure, propose a small paid stage: analysis, a prototype or the first feature. You will see how the company actually works: whether it meets deadlines, how it communicates, how good the result is.

## Common selection mistakes

- **Choosing by price alone.** Cheap development can end up costing more due to rework.
- **Choosing by design portfolio.** Beautiful mockups say nothing about code quality and reliability.
- **No written scope.** Verbal agreements get forgotten.
- **Code and access stay with the vendor.** Switching later becomes hard.
- **Post-launch support not discussed.** Bugs and updates will come.

## What to put in the contract

- scope of work and acceptance criteria;
- stages and payments tied to deliverables;
- transfer of source code, domains, servers and accounts to the client;
- warranty period and support terms;
- change request procedure;
- confidentiality.

## FAQ

### Freelancer, agency or in-house team?

A freelancer fits small, clearly defined tasks. An agency fits when you need several roles at once and accountability for the result. An in-house team makes sense when the product is the core of the business and development is continuous.

### How many companies should I consider?

A few that passed initial screening is enough. Deeply comparing many candidates takes a lot of time and rarely changes the final choice.

### Does the vendor need to be in my city?

Not necessarily, but a shared language, time zone and understanding of the local market (payment systems, regulations, user habits) make the work noticeably easier.
