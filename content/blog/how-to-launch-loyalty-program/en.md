## The short answer

A loyalty program works when it has **one clear goal**, **a simple mechanic** and **calculated economics**. A launch looks like this:

1. Define the goal and the metric.
2. Choose a mechanic that serves this goal.
3. Calculate what rewards cost and when they pay off.
4. Integrate the program with the website, POS and CRM.
5. Measure results against a control group.

## Step 1. The goal

A loyalty program is not "nice to have". State what should change:

- **purchase frequency** — customers come back more often;
- **average order value** — they buy more at once;
- **retention** — fewer customers leave after their first purchase;
- **customer data** — more contacts and purchase history for personalized offers.

Pick one main goal. It defines the mechanic and how you will know the program works.

## Step 2. The mechanic

| Mechanic | How it works | Goal it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Bonus points | Part of the amount comes back as points for future purchases | Purchase frequency |
| Tiers (statuses) | The more you buy, the higher your tier and perks | Retention, order value |
| Cashback or member discount | A fixed benefit for members | Simplicity, acquisition |
| Paid membership | The customer pays for free delivery or special prices | Frequency in recurring categories |
| Referral program | A bonus for inviting a friend | Acquiring new customers |

Start with one mechanic. The rules should fit in a couple of sentences: if customers do not understand how to get the benefit, the program will not change their behavior.

## Step 3. The economics

Rewards are a cost, so calculate them before launch. Key factors:

- **Margin** by product category — a reward must not eat the profit on low-margin items.
- **Earn rate** and limits: how much of an order can be paid with points.
- **Redemption share** — not all points will be spent, but counting on expiry ("breakage") as a profit source is risky.
- **Points lifetime** — it encourages a return, but too short a period annoys people.
- **Launch and running costs**: development or a service subscription, communications, staff time.

The program pays off when the extra profit from changed member behavior exceeds the cost of rewards and operations. Model several scenarios — cautious, expected and optimistic.

## Step 4. Technical integration

What needs to be connected:

- **Customer account on the website** — balance, tier, earning history.
- **Cart and checkout** — redeeming points, calculating discounts, checking limits.
- **CRM** — a single customer profile, segments, triggered messages.
- **Offline POS**, if you have retail stores, so points work the same everywhere.
- **Accounting system** — correct recording of discounts and bonuses.

Important technical details: award points only after the order is paid and the return period has passed, handle returns and cancellations correctly, and protect against abuse (duplicate accounts, fake referrals).

The choice: a **ready-made loyalty service** connects faster to popular platforms, while **custom development** gives flexible rules and full control over the data.

## Step 5. Measuring results

The main trap is comparing members with all other customers. Already loyal shoppers are the ones who join, so their numbers will always look better.

How to measure honestly:

- Keep a **control group** that is not offered the program, or is offered it later.
- Compare purchase frequency, order value and retention between the groups.
- Include the cost of rewards in the final profit, not just revenue growth.
- Adjust the rules based on results, not on gut feeling.

## FAQ

### What is better for a small store: points or a discount?

For a start, a simple mechanic is often enough — bonus points or a permanent member discount. Points give a reason to return, a discount is easier to understand. Choose by goal and margin.

### Can I launch a loyalty program without development?

Yes, if your store platform supports ready-made loyalty modules or services. Custom development is needed when the rules are non-standard or you want one program across the website, offline stores and an app.

### How soon will I see results?

It depends on how often customers buy in your category. It makes sense to evaluate after several typical repeat-purchase cycles, not a week after launch.
