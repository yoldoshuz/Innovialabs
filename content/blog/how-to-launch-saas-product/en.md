## The short answer

Launching SaaS is not "build the product and publish it". The order is: **validation → MVP → payments → onboarding → support → first customers**. The main early risk is not technical but market-related: building something nobody pays for.

## 1. Validate the idea before building

- **State the problem** in one sentence: who suffers and from what.
- **Talk to potential customers**: how they solve the problem today and what it costs them in time and money.
- **Test willingness to pay**: a landing page with a price and sign-up form, pre-orders, a paid pilot.
- **Study the alternatives** — competitors, Excel, manual work. If the alternative is "it's fine as is", selling will be hard.

If no one asks for access after dozens of conversations, change the hypothesis instead of writing code.

## 2. Build a narrow MVP

An MVP solves **one key problem** for **one customer segment**. Everything else comes later.

What the first version usually needs:

- sign-up and login;
- the core feature people pay for;
- basic account settings;
- payments (see below);
- usage analytics to understand user behavior.

Better to avoid: dozens of integrations, complex roles and permissions, a mobile app "just in case".

Think through **multi-tenancy** (separating different customers' data) from the start — reworking it later is expensive.

## 3. Billing

Payments should work from the very first paying customer. Decide on:

- **pricing model**: tiered subscription, per user, per usage;
- **billing period**: monthly and annual (annual usually comes with a discount);
- **payment provider**: services like Stripe for international markets, local systems like Payme and Click for Uzbekistan;
- **a free trial** or a free plan;
- what happens when a payment fails: retries, notifications, restricted access.

Do not build billing entirely yourself if you can use the payment provider's ready-made subscriptions.

## 4. Onboarding

Users should reach their **first value** as fast as possible.

- minimal fields at sign-up;
- a step-by-step setup wizard or demo data;
- in-app hints at key steps;
- emails or messages that bring back people who stopped halfway.

Use analytics to see at which step people abandon the product and improve that step first.

## 5. Support

Early on, support is your main source of knowledge about the product.

- chat on the website or in Telegram, email;
- a knowledge base with answers to frequent questions;
- fast replies — the founder talks to the first customers personally;
- a system for collecting feedback and feature requests.

## 6. First customers

Advertising almost never brings the first customers. What works:

- **direct sales**: personal messages, calls and meetings with the people you validated the idea with;
- **professional communities** and industry chats;
- **content**: articles and breakdowns of the problems the product solves;
- **partnerships** with companies that share your customers;
- **referrals** from happy users.

Metrics worth tracking: activation, retention, churn and monthly recurring revenue (MRR).

## Common mistakes

- Building for a long time without talking to customers.
- Launching without payments "to grow users first".
- Building a product for everyone at once.
- Ignoring churn and watching only sign-ups.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to launch a SaaS?

It depends on the complexity of the core feature, integrations and team size. A narrow MVP and ready-made services for auth, payments and email shorten the timeline.

### Do I need a free plan at launch?

Not necessarily. A free trial is often simpler: you learn faster whether people are willing to pay. A free plan makes sense when the product spreads on its own through users.

### When should I scale marketing?

When retention is stable and customers stay after the first months. If users leave, advertising only speeds up losing money.
