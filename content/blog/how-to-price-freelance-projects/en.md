## Hourly or fixed price: the short answer

- **Hourly** — when the task is fuzzy, requirements will change or the work is ongoing (support, improvements, consulting).
- **Fixed price** — when the scope is clear and written down: a landing page from a finished design, a bot with a specific feature list.

The key rule: **a price without a defined scope is a lottery**. Whatever model you choose, you and the client must understand exactly what will be delivered in the same way.

## Comparing the models

| | Hourly | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Risk of underestimating | On the client | On you |
| Budget clarity for the client | Lower | Higher |
| Flexibility with changes | High | Needs re-estimation |
| What you maintain | Time tracking and reports | A clear spec and milestones |
| Best for | Support, R&D, vague tasks | Typical projects with clear scope |

There are middle-ground options too: **fixed price per phase** (a paid discovery and prototype first, then an estimate for development) or a **retainer** — a fixed monthly block of hours for a regular client.

## How to estimate scope

1. **Break the project into tasks** of a few hours each: "authentication" is too big; "login form", "password reset" and "validation" are better.
2. **Count the invisible work**: calls, messages, environment setup, deployment, testing, revisions, documentation. This is what usually eats the time.
3. **Estimate as a range**: an optimistic and a pessimistic figure per task. A wide gap signals that the task needs clarifying.
4. **Compare with your history**: track actual time on your projects — it is the best source for future estimates.
5. **Ask questions before quoting**: integrations, number of user roles, content, who prepares texts and images.

## A buffer for risk

An estimate without a buffer almost always turns out too low. The buffer is not padding; it is the price of uncertainty. Its size depends on:

- how detailed the requirements are;
- whether you have worked with this stack or API before;
- third-party dependencies (someone else's backend, access, approvals);
- how quickly the client responds and makes decisions.

The more unknowns, the bigger the buffer — or the stronger the case for hourly billing or a separate paid discovery phase.

## Handling scope creep

**Scope creep** is when "small requests" gradually turn the project into a different one. How to protect yourself:

- **Write down what is out of scope**, not only what is in.
- **Limit revision rounds** in the contract.
- Answer each new request calmly and specifically: "Good idea; it was not part of the original scope, so I will estimate it separately."
- **Keep extra tasks in a backlog** and offer them as the next phase instead of doing them silently.
- Split payment by milestones so changes naturally go into the next one.

## How to raise your rates

- Review your rate regularly, for example when starting a new project or at a set interval.
- Quote **new clients** the new rate straight away; give **existing clients** advance notice.
- Raise the price along with your value: specialisation, case studies with results, speed, reliable communication.
- Move from selling hours to selling outcomes: packages and fixed-scope products let you earn from your own efficiency.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a price in the first message without asking a single question.
- Undercutting to "get in" with a client — raising the price later is hard.
- Ignoring platform fees, bank fees and taxes.
- Agreeing to a fixed price on a vague spec.

## FAQ

### Should a beginner choose hourly or fixed pricing?

For small, clear tasks a fixed price is easier to sell. If requirements are vague, offer hourly billing with a cap or a paid first phase, after which you can give an accurate estimate.

### What if I underestimated a fixed-price project?

If the mistake is yours, it is usually fairer to finish the agreed scope and apply the lesson to future estimates. If the requirements changed, that is grounds for re-estimating — raise it immediately, not at the end.

### How do I tell a client about a rate increase?

Briefly and in advance: the date the new rate applies and what stays the same. Letting current phases finish at the old price helps preserve trust.
