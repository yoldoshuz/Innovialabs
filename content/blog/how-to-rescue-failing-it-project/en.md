## The short answer

A failing project is rescued by **pausing and diagnosing**, not by speeding up. Adding people or demanding overtime is the most common move and almost never works. The order of actions is:

1. Establish the real state of the project.
2. Find the root cause, not the symptoms.
3. Cut the scope to the smallest valuable result.
4. Renegotiate timelines based on facts.
5. Switch to short cycles with regular demos.

## Signs a project is in trouble

- Deadlines have slipped more than once, and nobody takes new estimates seriously.
- "Almost done" has been the status for weeks.
- Bugs are piling up faster than they are fixed.
- The client and the team communicate through complaints rather than tasks.
- Nobody can say clearly what goes into the first release.

If two or three of these sound familiar, it is time to act instead of waiting for things to fix themselves.

## Step 1. Audit the real state

Collect facts, not opinions:

- **What actually works** — check it on a test environment, not in reports.
- **Code health** — are there tests, documentation, a clear architecture? If in doubt, order an independent technical audit.
- **Access and assets** — repository, servers, domains, service accounts. All of it should belong to the client.
- **Backlog** — how many tasks remain and how well they are described.

## Step 2. Find the root cause

Symptoms look alike; causes differ. The cure depends on the cause.

| Root cause | How it shows | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Vague requirements | Constant rework, "we understood it differently" | Fix requirements and acceptance criteria |
| Scope creep | Tasks are added faster than they are closed | Prioritize hard, move the rest to later phases |
| Weak team or missing skills | Low quality, slow progress | Strengthen key roles or change the vendor |
| Technical debt | Every change breaks something else | Set aside time for stabilization and tests |
| No ownership | Nobody owns priorities and deadlines | Appoint one product owner |
| Poor communication | Surprises on every call | Regular demos and a single status channel |

Usually there are several causes. Start with the one that blocks the others.

## Step 3. Reset the scope

Answer one question: **what is the smallest result that delivers business value?** Everything outside that answer moves to later.

A practical technique is to split the backlog into three groups:

- **must have for launch** — the product is pointless without it;
- **important, but after launch**;
- **nice to have** — postponed without regret.

Cutting scope is not a defeat. It is a way to get a working product sooner and start learning from real users.

## Step 4. Renegotiate timelines

The new plan is based on the team's actual pace, not the desired one. To keep the estimate honest:

- break the remaining work into small pieces;
- reserve time for stabilization and testing;
- agree on checkpoints where the plan is reviewed.

If scope or deadlines change, put it in writing — as a contract amendment or an updated plan.

## Step 5. Rebuild trust

Trust comes back through **visible progress**, not promises:

- short iterations with a demo of working results;
- transparent status: what is done, what is in progress, what the risks are;
- bad news is shared immediately, not the day before a deadline;
- one accountable party people can go to with questions.

## What not to do

- **Adding people to a late project** — newcomers need time to ramp up, and the team needs time to onboard them.
- **Looking for someone to blame** instead of causes.
- **Quietly carrying on** with the old plan and hoping to make it.
- **Switching vendors without access and documentation** — the new team will start from zero.

## FAQ

### When is it better to change the vendor rather than rescue the project with the current one?

When the root cause lies in the vendor's skills or reliability, and an honest conversation does not change the situation. Before switching, secure all access, code and documentation.

### Should the project be started from scratch?

Only if the audit shows the current foundation cannot be developed further. More often it pays to stabilize what exists and launch a reduced version.

### How long does it take to get a project back on track?

It depends on the cause and the scale of the problems. The first signs of improvement usually show up after a few short iterations, once scope is cut and the process becomes transparent.
