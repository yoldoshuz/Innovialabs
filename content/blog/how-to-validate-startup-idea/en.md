## The short answer: what "validating an idea" means

Validating an idea means getting proof that **people really want this problem solved and are willing to give something for it**: time, contact details or money. "Cool idea" from friends is not proof. You need **actions** from potential customers.

The good news: you almost never need a finished product for this. Below are four methods, from the cheapest to the more involved.

## Method 1. Customer interviews

The goal of an interview is to understand **the problem**, not to sell a solution. Talk to people from your target audience and ask about past experience, not future intentions.

Good questions:

- When did you last run into this problem?
- How do you solve it today?
- What annoys you most about the current solution?
- How much time or money does it cost you?

Bad questions: "Would you use an app like this?", "How much would you pay?". People answer those politely, not honestly.

**Success signal:** the problem is recurring and painful, and people already spend effort or money on it.

## Method 2. Landing page test

Build a one-page site that describes the product as if it already exists: problem, solution, benefits and a call to action — "Request access", "Join the waitlist", "Get pricing".

Then drive traffic to it: ads, niche Telegram channels, communities. Look not at page views but at **conversion to action**: how many visitors left their contact details.

You can build several versions with different positioning and compare which message resonates best.

## Method 3. Pre-sales

The strongest signal is **money**. Offer to sell the product in advance: at a discount, as a pre-order or as a pilot contract for B2B.

- For B2C — prepayment or a deposit for early access.
- For B2B — a letter of intent or a paid pilot.

If people pay for something that does not exist yet, demand is confirmed far more reliably than by any survey. Be honest about timelines and refund terms.

## Method 4. No-code experiments

Sometimes value has to be shown in action. Assemble a working version without programming:

- **website and form builders** to collect requests;
- **spreadsheets** instead of a database;
- a **Telegram bot** built with a ready-made builder;
- **manual work** behind the scenes — the "Wizard of Oz" approach, where the user sees a service and you run the process by hand.

This tests not only demand but the scenario itself: is it clear, where do people get stuck, what do they actually need.

## How to choose a method

| Method | What it tests | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Interviews | Whether the problem exists | Time only |
| Landing page | Whether the solution is interesting | Low |
| Pre-sales | Whether people will pay | Low |
| No-code | Whether the scenario works | Medium |

They are usually done in order: problem first, then interest, then money.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking only people you know.** They do not want to upset you.
- **Falling in love with the solution.** Test the problem; the solution can change.
- **Not setting a success criterion upfront.** Decide before the experiment what result counts as confirmation.
- **Ordering development right away.** Code is the most expensive way to test a hypothesis.

## FAQ

### How many interviews should I run?

There is no fixed number. Keep going until answers start repeating and you stop learning anything new about the problem. This usually becomes clear after a series of conversations with the same audience.

### Will someone steal my idea if I talk about it?

An idea without execution is worth little. The risk of spending resources on a product nobody needs is much higher than the risk of being copied after a conversation.

### What if validation shows there is no demand?

That is a good outcome: you saved your budget. Go back to interviews, refine the problem or the audience and test a new version of the hypothesis.
