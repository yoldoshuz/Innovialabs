## What a brief must contain

A **design brief** is a short document that pins down the task before work starts. It is not paperwork for the designer. It protects you: without it, every round of feedback turns into an argument about taste.

A good brief answers six questions:

1. **Goal** — which business problem the design solves.
2. **Audience** — who will use the site or see the logo.
3. **Competitors** — who you will be compared against.
4. **References** — what you like and dislike, and above all, why.
5. **Deliverables** — which files and layouts you get at the end.
6. **Constraints** — deadlines, budget, technical and legal limits.

If any question is left blank, the designer will have to guess the answer.

## How to answer each question

**Goal.** Describe an outcome, not a look. "We need a modern website" is a look. "We want visitors to book a consultation instead of calling to ask about prices" is a goal you can design for.

**Audience.** Describe one to three typical users: who they are, which device they use, what they want to do and what stops them. Context matters more than age or gender: "a procurement manager comparing three suppliers on a laptop during work hours."

**Competitors.** Share three to five links and note how you differ. For a logo this is critical: your mark should not look like the marks of your market neighbours.

**References.** A link without a comment is almost useless. Write "we like the large type and generous white space, we dislike dark backgrounds." References can come from any industry.

**Deliverables.** List concrete outputs: number of pages or screens, mobile version, UI kit, Figma source files; for a logo, the primary and simplified versions, vector files and a usage guide.

**Constraints.** Deadline, budget range, existing colours or fonts that must stay, CMS requirements, site languages and legal points such as trademarks and licences for fonts and photos.

## Brief template

Copy it and fill it in. If you do not know an answer, write "not sure" — that is useful information too.

```markdown
## About the project
- Company and product (2-3 sentences):
- What we need: website / logo / both
- Why now (what changed):

## Goal
- Main action we want from the user:
- How we will know the design works:

## Audience
- User 1 (task, context, device):
- User 2:
- What stops or worries them:

## Competitors
- Link — how we differ:
- Link — how we differ:

## References
- Like (link — why):

- Three words the brand should feel like:

## Deliverables
- Pages / screens:
- Mobile version: yes / no
- File formats and source files:

## Constraints
- Deadline:
- Budget range:
- What cannot change (colours, fonts, name):
- Technical requirements (CMS, languages, integrations):

## Contacts
- Who makes the final decision:
- Who gives feedback:
```

## Common mistakes

- **A ten-page brief.** The longer the document, the less carefully it gets read. One or two pages is enough.
- **"Make it like Apple."** Without saying what exactly you like, this tells the designer nothing.
- **No single decision-maker.** When feedback comes from five people with different opinions, the project slows down more than from any other cause.
- **A hidden budget.** A range helps the designer propose a realistic scope instead of an ideal but unreachable one.
- **A brief that never changes.** If the goal shifts mid-project, write the change down.

## FAQ

### Do I need a brief for a small project?

Yes, just a shorter one. Even for a single page or an icon, write down the goal, audience, a couple of references and the deadline. It takes minutes and saves rounds of revisions.

### Who should fill in the brief, the client or the designer?

Usually the client writes a draft, and the designer asks clarifying questions on a call and completes the document. Both sides should agree on the final version.

### How is a logo brief different from a website brief?

A logo brief focuses more on positioning, brand personality and competitors, and its deliverables include versions of the mark and usage rules. A website brief puts more weight on user scenarios, page structure and technical requirements.
