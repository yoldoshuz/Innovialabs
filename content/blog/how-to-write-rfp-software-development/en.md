## What an RFP is and when you need one

An **RFP (Request for Proposal)** is a document you send to several vendors to get comparable proposals for a project. You need it when you are choosing between several companies and want to compare answers on the same parameters.

The main purpose of an RFP is to give vendors **enough information for an accurate estimate**. The fewer unknowns, the less "risk buffer" goes into the price and the closer the answers are to one another.

## RFP structure

### 1. About the company and the project

Briefly: what the company does, why the project is needed and which business problem it solves. State how you will measure success.

### 2. Users and scenarios

Who will use the system and what exactly they will do. Scenarios like "a manager receives a request, assigns an executor, the customer sees the status" give a vendor more than an abstract feature list.

### 3. Functional requirements

List the features and mark the priority of each: required for launch, desirable, can come later. This lets vendors propose a phased plan.

### 4. Non-functional requirements

- expected load and number of users;
- security and data storage requirements;
- interface languages;
- platforms: web, iOS, Android;
- accessibility and performance requirements.

### 5. Integrations and current infrastructure

Which systems already exist: CRM, accounting software, payment services, inventory. Do they have APIs, and who maintains them?

### 6. Constraints

Desired timeline, budget range (at least a bracket), and technology preferences if they are justified — for example, your in-house team will maintain the product on a specific stack.

### 7. What you expect in the response

Set **one response format**, otherwise comparison becomes hard:

- understanding of the problem in their own words;
- proposed solution and stack;
- plan by phases and timeline;
- cost estimate broken down by phase;
- team composition;
- relevant experience;
- payment model and support terms;
- assumptions and risks.

### 8. Selection process and timeline

Deadline for questions, response deadline, decision date and contact person.

## How to make estimates more accurate

- **Attach materials:** mockups, examples, process diagrams.
- **Allow questions** and share the answers with all participants — it levels the playing field.
- **Ask vendors to list assumptions.** Price differences are often explained by companies understanding the scope differently.
- **Do not demand a fixed price** if requirements are still rough. Ask for a range plus the cost of a discovery phase instead.

## How to score responses

Build a table of weighted criteria before you receive the answers. An example set:

| Criterion | What to check |
|---|---|
| Understanding | Did the vendor restate your problem, not a template? |
| Solution quality | Is the stack justified, are integrations covered? |
| Experience | Are there similar projects you can actually see? |
| Team | Who specifically will work on the project? |
| Timeline and plan | Realistic, with intermediate deliverables? |
| Cost | Transparent breakdown, what is included? |
| Process | Reporting, communication, handling change |

Each reviewer scores independently, then the results are combined. You choose the weights based on what is most critical for your project.

## Common mistakes

- **A document that is too generic** — the responses will not be comparable.
- **A document that is too detailed**, with a ready-made architecture — you lose the vendors' expertise.
- **Choosing on price alone.** The cheapest proposal often simply includes less work.
- **Sending it to dozens of companies.** Comparing many responses properly is hard; it is wiser to build a shortlist first.

## FAQ

### How many vendors should I invite?

A shortlist of a few companies, pre-selected by portfolio and references, is usually enough. You still have a choice, and comparison does not take weeks.

### Should I state a budget in the RFP?

It helps to give at least a range. Without it, vendors propose solutions of completely different scale, and they are hard to compare.

### How is an RFP different from a specification?

An RFP is for choosing a vendor and describes the problem in enough detail to estimate it. A detailed specification is usually prepared together with the chosen vendor.
