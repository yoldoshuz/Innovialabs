## Two paths after senior

After the senior level, an IT career usually branches:

- **The management track**: engineering manager, head of a department. Results come through people: hiring, development, organizing work.
- **The individual contributor (IC) track**: staff engineer, principal engineer. Results come through technical decisions that affect several teams or the whole system.

Many companies treat the two tracks as equal in level. It is not a ladder where the manager sits above the engineer, but two different professions.

## What the work looks like

| Aspect | Engineering manager | Staff engineer |
|---|---|---|
| Main work | 1:1s, hiring, planning, solving team problems | Architecture, technical documents, hard problems |
| Code | Little or none | Some, often prototypes and critical parts |
| Calendar | Many meetings, frequent context switches | More deep work time, though still plenty of meetings |
| Scope of influence | One team or several teams | Several teams, company technical direction |
| Feedback | Slow: results show up over months | Also slow: decisions are tested by time |

## Success metrics

**A manager** is judged by the team:

- the team delivers consistently;
- people grow and don't leave without reason;
- problems are spotted and resolved on time;
- hiring brings in the right people.

**A staff engineer** is judged by technical influence:

- key decisions hold up as load and product grow;
- hard cross-team problems get solved;
- other engineers grow thanks to their standards and mentoring;
- technical debt is managed rather than piling up uncontrolled.

## Risks of each track

**Management:**

- technical skills gradually go stale;
- less of the "I built this myself" feeling;
- emotional load: conflicts, layoffs, difficult conversations;
- there are fewer manager openings than engineering ones.

**IC track:**

- small companies may have no staff-level positions;
- you have to influence without formal authority, through persuasion;
- expectations are vague and need active clarification;
- the risk of getting stuck as "the most experienced developer" without truly broader impact.

## Questions to help you choose

Answer honestly, ideally in writing:

1. What satisfies you at the end of the day: a solved technical problem or helping a person?
2. How do you feel about frequent context switching and a packed calendar?
3. Are you ready for hard conversations about weak performance, conflicts or letting someone go?
4. Do you want to stay deep in technology a few years from now?
5. Do you enjoy influencing through persuasion, written arguments and long discussions?
6. Which roles actually exist in your company or the market you work in?

If your answers contradict each other, that's normal. The decision doesn't have to be final.

## How to try before deciding

- **Management**: run a few 1:1s with junior colleagues, take on onboarding, help with hiring.
- **IC track**: take on a cross-team technical problem, write a design document and lead its review.

Discuss the experiment with your manager. Many companies support this kind of trial.

## FAQ

### Can I move back from management to engineering?

Yes, this move happens and is considered normal. The longer you are out of hands-on work, the longer it takes to rebuild technical skills, so it helps to stay in touch with the code.

### Which track pays more?

It depends on the company and market. In many companies the levels of both tracks are comparable, so rely on your company's internal grading system rather than general assumptions.

### What if my company has no IC track?

Ask your manager how the company sees growth for strong engineers without moving into management. If there is no such path and it matters to you, consider companies with an explicit IC track.
