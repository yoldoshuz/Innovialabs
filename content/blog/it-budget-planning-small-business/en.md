## The short answer

A small business IT budget is made of **five lines**: software subscriptions, development and improvements, hardware, support and security, and a reserve. Plan it a year ahead with a monthly breakdown, and start not with a list of tools but with a question: which business processes should become faster, cheaper or more reliable.

## Step 1. Take stock of what you already have

Before adding new costs, put everything current into one table:

- all subscriptions and services, who pays for them and from which card;
- domains, hosting, cloud services and their renewal dates;
- computers, phones, network equipment and their age;
- contractors and staff who handle IT;
- licenses that renew automatically.

This step almost always reveals **duplicate services** and subscriptions nobody uses.

## Step 2. The five budget lines

### Software subscriptions (SaaS)

CRM, accounting, email and office suite, messengers, cloud storage, mailing services. Pay attention to **per-user pricing**: when you hire, this line grows automatically. Check whether annual billing is cheaper than monthly.

### Development and improvements

A website, an online store, a Telegram bot, integrations between systems, report automation. Improvements to existing solutions belong here too. Separate **one-off projects** from **ongoing improvements**: the former are planned around a specific goal, the latter as a monthly hours allowance.

### Hardware

Workstations, laptops, printers, point-of-sale equipment, routers. Hardware ages, so it helps to define a **replacement cycle** for each device type in advance and spread replacements across years instead of renewing everything at once.

### Support and security

A system administrator or outsourced support, website hosting and maintenance, backups, antivirus, training staff in basic digital hygiene. This line looks optional until something breaks.

### Reserve

Unexpected costs always happen: a server fails, the exchange rate moves, a service raises its prices, an urgent change is needed. A reserve keeps you from pausing planned projects to cover emergencies.

## A sample budget structure

Here is an example of what an IT budget table can look like. You fill in the amounts yourself: they depend on team size, industry and the services you choose.

| Line | What it covers | Cost type | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subscriptions | CRM, email, accounting, storage | Operating | Monthly / annual |
| Development | New website, CRM-to-site integration | Project | By project milestones |
| Improvements | Small enhancements, fixes | Operating | Monthly allowance |
| Hardware | Laptops, POS, network | Capital | By replacement schedule |
| Support | Admin, hosting, backups | Operating | Monthly |
| Security | Antivirus, training, access review | Operating | Monthly / annual |
| Reserve | Urgent repairs, price increases | Reserve | As needed |

## How to set priorities

1. **Essential to operate** — what stops the business if it fails: POS, email, accounting, the website that takes orders.
2. **Protection** — backups, access control, security.
3. **Growth** — projects that save staff time or bring customers.
4. **Convenience** — everything else.

For each growth project, write down the **expected effect**: how many work hours it saves or which process it speeds up. This makes projects comparable.

## Common mistakes

- **No budget owner.** Different employees sign up for subscriptions, and nobody sees the full picture.
- **Only one-off spending.** The website was ordered, but nothing was set aside for hosting, updates and support.
- **Currency risk.** Many services are billed in foreign currency — account for that when planning.
- **No reviews.** The budget was written once and never opened again. Compare plan and actuals at least quarterly.

## FAQ

### What share of revenue should go to IT?

There is no universal figure: it depends on the industry, business model and level of digitization. It is more reliable to start from tasks — which processes need support and improvement — and check whether the investment pays off.

### Is an in-house IT person or outsourcing better?

For a small company with typical tasks, outsourcing is usually more flexible: you pay for the work you need. An in-house specialist makes sense when there is a lot of ongoing work that requires deep knowledge of internal processes.

### How often should the IT budget be reviewed?

Comparing plan and actuals quarterly works well, with a full rebuild once a year before the new financial period. An unplanned review is needed when the team grows or you launch a new line of business.
