## The short answer

**Yes, you can.** In development, testing, design and analytics, employers mostly check what you can actually do: a take-home task, a technical interview, your code and your projects. A degree is usually a bonus, not a hard requirement.

That does not mean it is equally easy everywhere. Some companies and situations make the path longer or formally closed without a diploma.

## Where a degree barely matters

- **Product companies and startups.** Results decide: can you ship tasks and work in a team.
- **Agencies and outsourcing studios.** They look at your stack, a test task and experience on similar projects.
- **Freelancing.** Clients care about your portfolio, reviews and deadlines.
- **Roles with visible output:** frontend, mobile development, UI/UX design, QA. Your work is easy to show.

## Where a degree still carries weight

- **Government bodies and banks.** A higher-education requirement is often written into hiring rules.
- **Large corporations with formal HR filters.** A résumé without a degree can be filtered out automatically, even if your skills fit.
- **Work visas and relocation.** Many countries take a relevant degree into account when issuing work visas, or ask for more years of experience without one. If you plan to move, check the rules of the specific country early.
- **Research-heavy fields:** machine learning research, computer vision, cryptography. These value the math foundation a university usually provides.

## How to compensate for not having a degree

### A portfolio people can verify

Two or three finished projects beat ten tutorial clones. A strong portfolio:

- solves a clear problem (a booking bot, a user dashboard, a landing page with a form);
- lives in a public repository with a README: what it is, how to run it, which stack;
- is deployed and opens from a link;
- shows growth: early and recent projects differ in quality.

### Certifications as a supplement

Certificates from cloud providers, vendors and recognized platforms help you pass HR filters and give your learning structure. On their own they do not replace experience. They work best in pairs: a certificate plus a project where you applied that knowledge.

### Real experience at any scale

- internships and junior programs;
- small projects for businesses you know;
- open source contributions, even small ones: docs, bug fixes;
- hackathons and team projects.

## Common mistakes

- **Endless learning instead of building.** Courses without your own projects give you nothing to discuss in interviews.
- **Hiding the missing degree.** Be upfront about self-education and lead with your skills.
- **Applying only to big corporations.** It is easier to start where you are judged by a test task.
- **Skipping fundamentals.** Algorithms, networking, databases and OS basics come up whether you have a degree or not.

## How to build your path

1. Pick one role and one stack and stay focused.
2. Take a structured program or build your own plan from official documentation.
3. Ship 2–3 projects you are proud to show.
4. Build your résumé around skills and projects.
5. Apply where there are test tasks, and ask for feedback after rejections.

## FAQ

### Should I get a degree if I already work in IT?

Only if you have a specific goal: relocating on a work visa, working in the public sector or moving into research. For growth inside the industry, experience and the complexity of problems you have solved matter more.

### What should I put under "Education" on my résumé?

List what you have: a non-IT degree, courses, self-study programs. Keep this section short and place it after experience and projects.

### Does a non-IT degree help?

Sometimes. A background in economics, engineering or medicine is valued on projects in those industries, and having any higher education helps you pass formal filters.
