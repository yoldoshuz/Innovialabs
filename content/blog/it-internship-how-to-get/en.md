## The short answer

An internship is the shortest path from learning to your first job. Getting one takes three things: **knowing where companies look for interns**, **showing that you can already build something**, and **going through selection prepared and calm**. Work experience is not required. Proof that you learn fast and finish what you start is.

## Where internships are announced

- **Company career pages.** Larger companies run intern programs in cohorts, often once or twice a year. Subscribe to their updates and track application deadlines.
- **Telegram channels and job chats** for juniors. In Uzbekistan and across the CIS this is one of the main hiring channels.
- **Job boards and LinkedIn**: filter by "intern", "internship" or "trainee".
- **Universities, courses and hackathons.** Companies often come there looking for interns themselves.
- **A direct email.** Small studios and startups do not always post openings. A short message with your portfolio and a concrete question like "do you need an intern in X" works better than you might expect.
- **People and community.** Meetups, open source and developer chats help: a referral from a developer you know shortens the path noticeably.

## How selection works

The stages vary, but usually it is a mix of these:

| Stage | What it checks |
|---|---|
| Application and resume | Basic skills, motivation, attention to detail |
| Online test or contest | Algorithms, logic, language fundamentals |
| Take-home task | Ability to bring a small task to a working result |
| Interview | How you think, explain and learn |

Nobody expects deep expertise from an intern. They expect **solid fundamentals**, **clear thinking out loud** and **genuine interest** in the field.

## How to prepare

1. **Pick one direction.** Frontend, backend, QA, design, analytics: "I will do anything" reads weaker than a clear choice.
2. **Build one or two finished projects.** One small project that works, is deployed and has a README beats five half-done ones.
3. **Clean up your GitHub and resume.** One page, skills, projects with links, contacts. No filler.
4. **Review the fundamentals.** Data structures, basic algorithms, HTTP, Git, SQL, depending on your direction.
5. **Prepare a one to two minute story about yourself:** what you studied, what you built, why this direction.
6. **Practice solving problems out loud.** Interviewers care about your reasoning, not only the final answer.

## How to turn an internship into an offer

An internship is one long interview. You are judged not by what you knew on day one but by **your progress**.

- **Finish your tasks.** A small task that is closed is worth more than a big one abandoned halfway.
- **Ask questions the right way.** Try to figure it out first, then come with specifics: what you tried, what failed, what you suspect.
- **Report your status.** If you are stuck or running late, say so early, not on the deadline.
- **Take code review calmly.** Comments are learning, not personal criticism. Do not repeat the same mistake twice.
- **Keep notes.** Write down what you learn about the project, processes and tools. It speeds up growth and helps in the final conversation.
- **Ask for feedback early.** Around the middle of the internship, ask your mentor what you need to improve to get an offer.
- **Discuss the next step directly.** Near the end, ask: "What would it take for me to stay on the team?" This is a normal professional conversation.

## Common mistakes

- Postponing applications until you "know everything". Apply as soon as you have the basics and one project.
- Sending the same resume everywhere with no cover note.
- Staying silent for a week while stuck on a task.
- Treating the internship as school rather than work: being late, skipping meetings, ignoring chats.

## FAQ

### Can I get an internship without a computer science degree?

Yes. Many companies look at skills and projects rather than diplomas. A finished project, a tidy GitHub and confident fundamentals often matter more than your major.

### What if the internship is unpaid?

Weigh what you get: real tasks, a mentor, code review, a path to hiring. If instead of learning you are asked to do the work of full-time staff for free and without guidance, that is a reason to decline.

### How many applications should I send?

There is no exact number; it depends on the field and the season. Rejections at the start are normal. Analyzing each attempt and improving your resume and preparation matters more than simply sending more applications.
