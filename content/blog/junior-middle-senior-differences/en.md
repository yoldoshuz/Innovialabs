## The short answer: levels are about autonomy and scope

A common myth is that levels depend on tenure: one year means junior, three means middle, five means senior. In practice, companies evaluate something else:

- **autonomy**: how much help you need to get a task done;
- **scope of responsibility**: a task, a feature, a system or a whole area;
- **decision-making**: following others' decisions, making your own or setting direction for others;
- **impact**: on your own code, on the product, on the team.

You can stay a junior in substance for five years if you spend them on repetitive tasks under constant supervision. And you can grow into a middle in a year and a half in a strong team with challenging work.

## Levels side by side

| Criterion | Junior | Middle | Senior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autonomy | needs help and regular check-ins | works independently, asks for help when it matters | works without supervision, helps others |
| Task scope | small, well-defined tasks | whole features, including unclear parts | systems, architecture, cross-cutting problems |
| Decisions | follows established decisions | chooses the approach within their task | makes decisions that affect the team and product |
| Handling ambiguity | waits for clarification | asks questions and clarifies on their own | turns a vague problem into a plan |
| Impact | their own code | their part of the product | the team, processes and technical direction |

## Junior: learning to do tasks right

A junior can already program but has little experience applying that knowledge in a real project.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- picks up a clearly described task and implements it, asking questions along the way;
- receives many comments in code review and learns from them;
- may stay stuck on a problem for a long time before asking; that is a normal stage, but learning to ask for help at the right moment matters;
- rarely thinks about consequences beyond the task: performance, security, maintainability.

**Growth signal:** fewer tasks bounce back from review, and questions become more specific.

## Middle: delivering independently

A middle is a reliable engineer who can be handed a feature without step-by-step supervision.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- receives "build report export" and figures out the details: format, data volume, access rights;
- notices conflicting requirements and raises the issue before starting, not after;
- writes code colleagues can understand and covers it with tests where it matters;
- leaves useful comments when reviewing others' code;
- estimates their own work realistically.

**Growth signal:** people start coming to you for advice, and your decisions reach beyond individual tasks.

## Senior: owning decisions and lifting the team

A senior is not "a middle who codes faster". The key difference is **scope of influence and ownership of the consequences of decisions**.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- proposes the architecture for a new system, lays out trade-offs and explains why this option was chosen;
- spots risks early: "this design will not handle data growth, let's plan for a different approach now";
- can say "let's not build this feature this way; here is a simpler option with the same business result";
- mentors less experienced colleagues and raises code quality across the team through reviews and standards;
- talks to the business and translates its goals into technical solutions.

A defining senior trait is **the ability to simplify**. A complex solution where a simple one would do is more often a sign of too little experience than too much.

## How to grow to the next level

- Take tasks slightly above your current level and see them through.
- Ask **why** a task exists, not only **how** to do it.
- Practice explaining your decisions in writing and out loud.
- Regularly ask senior colleagues for feedback on what you need to grow.
- Learn adjacent areas: how your code is deployed, tested and used.

Keep in mind that level criteria vary between companies: a senior at a small studio and a senior at a large corporation may own very different things. Clarify expectations with your manager.

## FAQ

### How many years does it take to become a senior?

There is no fixed timeline. Growth speed depends on task complexity, feedback quality and how deliberately you work, not on the calendar.

### Can I join as a middle without junior experience?

Sometimes, for people with strong experience in an adjacent field, such as a move from another engineering role. Usually, though, you need to prove your autonomy on real projects.

### What comes after senior?

The path usually branches: technical (lead, staff, principal, architect) or managerial (team lead, head of engineering). The titles and their content differ between companies.
