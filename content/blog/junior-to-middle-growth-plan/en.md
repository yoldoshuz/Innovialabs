## What separates middle from junior

The core signal of a middle developer is **independence**. A junior does well with a clearly defined task but often gets stuck when requirements are vague or something goes wrong. A middle developer takes a medium-sized task, clarifies the requirements, finds a solution and ships it to production.

Typical middle-level signals:

- **Decomposition**: breaks a feature into steps and estimates them reasonably well.
- **System understanding**: knows how their code connects to the database, APIs, deployment and monitoring.
- **Quality without reminders**: writes tests, handles errors, thinks about edge cases.
- **Debugging**: reads logs, reproduces bugs and finds the cause, not just the symptom.
- **Communication**: flags risks and delays early, asks focused questions.
- **Code review**: not only receives comments but gives useful ones to others.

Years of experience alone guarantee nothing. People get promoted for observable behavior.

## A 6 to 12 month plan

Split your growth into three stages. Timing depends on the project and team, so focus on outcomes rather than the calendar.

| Stage | Focus | Stage goal |
|---|---|---|
| Months 1–3 | Depth in your stack and codebase | Close tasks without constant hints |
| Months 4–6 | Whole tasks, from requirements to release | Own one feature end to end |
| Months 7–12 | Impact on the team | Review code, help newcomers, improve processes |

**Stage 1.** Learn how the project works: architecture, build, deployment, logs. Master the tools you use daily: Git, the debugger, the profiler. Aim to replace "how do I do this" questions with "which option is better" questions.

**Stage 2.** Ask your manager for a task you can own completely: clarify requirements, propose a solution, implement, test and release it. Before coding, write a short plan in the ticket. It trains your thinking and saves reviewers time.

**Stage 3.** Review your teammates' code regularly, take on onboarding a new hire, or improve one process: tests, CI or documentation.

## Feedback loops: don't grow blind

Without feedback it is easy to work on the wrong things. Build a few loops into your routine:

- **Regular 1:1s with your manager** every two to four weeks. Ask directly: "What stops me from looking like a middle developer?"
- **Code review analysis** once a month. Collect recurring comments and turn them into goals.
- **Task retrospectives** after big tasks. Note where your estimate was off and why.
- **Explicit criteria**. Ask for the company's level descriptions, or write a list of expectations together with your manager.

## Evidence to collect

A promotion is easier when you have facts. Keep a simple document and update it weekly:

```markdown
## 2026-03
- Task: CSV export for reports
- My role: clarified requirements, proposed the design, built and shipped it
- Outcome: feature in production, no rollbacks
- Learned: streaming large files
```

Save links to tickets, pull requests, peer feedback and incidents you resolved. In six months this file becomes the basis for your promotion conversation.

## Common mistakes

- **Spreading across technologies** instead of going deep in your working stack.
- **Staying silent when stuck.** Middle developers ask for help too, but after their own research and with a specific question.
- **Expecting the promotion to be noticed.** Talk about your goal openly.
- **Ignoring the non-technical side**: estimates, communication, documentation.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to go from junior to middle?

There is no single timeline. It depends on project complexity, the quality of mentoring and how deliberately you grow. Measure yourself against level criteria, not years.

### Do I need to change companies to become a middle developer?

Not necessarily. First discuss growth with your manager and ask for harder tasks. If there is no room to grow for a long time, changing teams or companies is a reasonable option.

### What if my company has no level descriptions?

Write a list of expectations together with your manager and record it in writing. It protects you from vague criteria at your next review.
