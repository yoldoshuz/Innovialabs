## What a semantic core is and why you need it

A **semantic core** is the list of search queries your audience uses to find what you offer, grouped by site pages. It answers two questions: **which pages you need** and **what each one should be about**. Without it, a site grows chaotically: pages compete with each other and important topics stay uncovered.

## Step 1. Seed queries

Start with **seeds** — short 1–3 word phrases describing your products, services and customer problems.

- List every category and service.
- Add synonyms and casual phrasings ("web development", "build a website", "make a site").
- Look at how customers phrase things in inquiries, chats and reviews.
- Study competitors' menus and headings.

## Step 2. Expansion

Each seed expands into dozens or hundreds of long-tail queries.

| Tool | Type | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Yandex Wordstat | Free | Volumes and expansions for Yandex |
| Google Keyword Planner | Free (needs a Google Ads account) | Ideas and volume ranges for Google |
| Google Search Console | Free | Queries your site already appears for |
| Search suggestions | Free | Real user phrasing |
| Key Collector | Paid | Bulk collection and processing |
| Serpstat, Semrush, Ahrefs | Paid | Competitor keywords, difficulty, volumes |

Free sources are enough for a small site. Paid tools save time on large projects and competitor analysis.

## Step 3. Cleaning

A raw list is often half junk. Remove:

- **Irrelevant queries**: "free", "DIY", "download" if you sell a service.
- **Other brands and cities** you don't serve.
- **Queries with zero exact-match volume** — check with operators, not just the broad number.
- **Duplicates** and word reorderings.

Build a **negative keyword list** — it is useful for paid search too.

## Step 4. Clustering

Queries are grouped so that each group can be targeted by **one page**. The most reliable approach is **SERP-based clustering**: if two queries share many URLs in the top 10, the search engine treats them as one topic.

- **Hard clustering** — queries join a group only if every pair shares enough URLs. More precise, more groups.
- **Soft clustering** — a link to the group's main query is enough. Fewer groups, higher risk of mixing intents.

Paid services and Key Collector can cluster by SERP. A small core can be grouped manually by meaning, then disputed pairs checked in the results.

Also respect **intent**: "buy a sofa" (commercial) and "how to choose a sofa" (informational) need different pages even though the words overlap.

## Step 5. Mapping to pages

Each cluster gets **one page**:

1. Commercial clusters — categories, services, product pages.
2. Informational clusters — blog posts and FAQ.
3. The cluster's main query goes into the title and H1; the rest appear naturally in body text and subheadings.
4. Clusters without a suitable page become a plan for new pages.

Keep everything in one table: cluster, queries, volume, intent, URL, status.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing only high-volume queries** — competition is fierce and conversion is often lower than for long-tail queries.
- **Several pages for one cluster** — cannibalization; they undermine each other.
- **Mixing intents** on a single page.
- **Building the core once and forgetting it** — demand changes, so update it.

## FAQ

### How many queries should a core have?

As many as needed to cover your real offering and customer questions. A landing page may need a few dozen; a store may need thousands.

### Can I build a core without paid tools?

Yes. Wordstat, Keyword Planner, Search Console and suggestions provide enough data for small and mid-size sites. Paid services speed up collection and clustering.

### Do I need a separate core for each language?

Yes. People search differently in each language, and direct translations of queries often don't match real phrasing. Research each language version separately.
