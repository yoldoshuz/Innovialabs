## The short answer

A professional logo is not drawn in one sitting. The process has clear stages: **brief → research → sketches → concepts → vector refinement → testing → final files**. Each stage narrows the options, so the final mark is chosen for reasons, not by taste alone.

## Stage 1. The brief

The brief captures what the designer needs to know before drawing anything:

- what the company does and for whom;
- **brand attributes**: three to five words describing the character (for example, reliable, fast, friendly);
- competitors and what the logo must not look like;
- where the logo will live: website, app icon, signage, packaging, merchandise;
- languages and scripts the name will appear in;
- constraints: existing colors, a name that cannot change, deadlines.

A vague brief leads to endless revisions. If the client cannot answer these questions, run a short workshop first.

## Stage 2. Research

- **Competitor audit**: collect logos in the niche and group them by type, color and style. Look for clichés to avoid and gaps to use.
- **Mood board**: references for shapes, typography and feeling, not logos to copy.
- **Name analysis**: letters with interesting shapes, length, possible abbreviations.
- **Trademark check**: before investing in a concept, do a basic search for similar registered marks. Final legal checks are a job for a lawyer.

## Stage 3. Sketching

Sketch **fast and in quantity**, on paper or a tablet. Explore different directions:

- **wordmark**: the name set in custom or modified type;
- **lettermark**: initials or one letter;
- **symbol**: an abstract or pictorial mark;
- **combination**: symbol plus wordmark.

Do not judge too early. Many weak sketches are the normal path to a few strong ideas. Then select the most promising directions.

## Stage 4. Concepts

Develop **two or three concepts** that are genuinely different, not variations of one idea. For each:

- a clean digital version in black and white first;
- a short rationale: why this form fits the brief;
- a mock-up in context: website header, app icon, business card, sign.

Presenting in context helps the client judge the logo as it will actually be seen, instead of as an isolated picture on a white slide.

## Stage 5. Vectorizing and refinement

The chosen concept is rebuilt as clean **vector** artwork in Illustrator, Figma, Affinity Designer or a similar tool.

- Use as few anchor points as possible; smooth curves without kinks.
- Build on a **grid** or simple geometry where it helps consistency.
- Apply **optical corrections**: round shapes slightly overshoot the baseline, thin strokes are adjusted so they do not look thinner than they are.
- **Kern** the wordmark by eye, not only by numbers.
- If the wordmark uses an existing font, check its license for logo use, or convert to custom letterforms.
- Outline all text and expand strokes in final files.

## Stage 6. Testing

A logo must survive real conditions:

- **Small sizes**: favicon, app icon, social avatar. Details disappear, thin lines vanish. Often a simplified version is needed.
- **One color**: black on white and white on black. If the logo only works with gradients or many colors, it will fail on stamps, embroidery and fax-like prints.
- **Backgrounds**: light, dark, photos, the brand color.
- **Shapes**: inside a circle or square for social media avatars.
- **Distance**: how it reads on a sign from far away.
- **Scripts**: if the name exists in Cyrillic and Latin, check both versions together.

## Stage 7. The final file set

A client should receive a structured package:

| Category | What to include |
|---|---|
| Versions | main (horizontal), stacked or vertical, symbol only, wordmark only |
| Color variants | full color, black, white (reversed) |
| Vector formats | SVG, PDF, EPS or AI for print and further editing |
| Raster formats | PNG with transparent background in several sizes |
| Small sizes | favicon set, app icon, social avatar |
| Color specs | HEX and RGB for screens, CMYK and Pantone if printing is planned |
| Guidelines | clear space, minimum size, allowed backgrounds, do's and don'ts |

Name files clearly, for example `brand-logo-horizontal-white.svg`, and sort them into folders by format.

## Common mistakes

- Starting in color before the shape works in black and white.
- Showing ten variations of one idea instead of different concepts.
- Testing only at large sizes.
- Delivering only a PNG, so the client cannot print or scale the logo.
- Using fonts without a license that covers logo use.

## FAQ

### How many logo concepts should a client see?

Usually two or three well-developed, different concepts. More options tend to scatter the decision instead of helping it.

### Why do I need SVG if I already have a PNG?

SVG is vector, so it scales to any size without losing quality and stays sharp on retina screens and in print. PNG is fixed in pixels and blurs when enlarged.

### What does logo cost and timing depend on?

On the number of concepts and revision rounds, the depth of research, whether a full brand guide is needed, and how many versions and formats must be delivered.
