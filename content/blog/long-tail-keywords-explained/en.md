## The short answer

Queries are grouped by search volume — how often people search for them:

- **Head terms:** short and broad. "website", "crm".
- **Mid-tail:** more specific. "website development", "crm for a store".
- **Long-tail:** long and specific. "next.js online store development with payme payments".

Each long-tail query is searched rarely, but there is a huge number of them. Together they form the **long tail** — a large share of all search demand.

Exact volume thresholds depend on the niche and region, so they are set relative to a specific topic rather than taken from a universal table.

## Why the long tail brings customers

**A specific query means a clear need.** Someone searching "crm" could be a student, a journalist or a buyer. Someone searching "crm for a car service with appointment booking" is almost certainly choosing a solution for their business.

**Less competition.** Big sites fight over broad terms. Narrow phrases often have no well-matched pages, so a new site can take top positions there.

**Better intent match.** A long query makes it easier to understand what the person needs and to give exactly that on the page.

**It grows with your content.** One good article or page usually collects not one but dozens of related long-tail queries.

## Examples by level

| Level | Example | What the person wants |
|---|---|---|
| Head | "telegram bot" | Unclear: learn, build, buy |
| Mid | "telegram bot for business" | Exploring options |
| Long-tail | "telegram bot for taking restaurant orders" | Looking for a specific solution |
| Long-tail | "how long does it take to build a delivery telegram bot" | Preparing to order |

## How to find long-tail keywords

1. **Search autocomplete.** Start typing your main query and note the suggested completions.
2. **"People also ask" and related searches** at the bottom of results.
3. **Keyword tools:** Google Keyword Planner, Yandex Wordstat and similar.
4. **Search Console and Yandex Webmaster.** They show real queries your site already appears for.
5. **Customer questions.** Emails, calls and support chats are a ready source of phrasing.
6. **Forums and communities** where your audience discusses its problems.

## How to use them on your site

- **Group them.** Related long-tail queries are usually covered by one page, not ten near-identical ones.
- **Answer specifically.** If the query is about restaurants, show the restaurant scenario, not a generic text about bots.
- **Use natural phrasing** in H2 headings, FAQs and examples.
- **Link pages together.** Narrow articles link to the main service page and strengthen it.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing only head terms.** A long fight for traffic that converts poorly.
- **A page for every phrase.** Thin duplicates dilute the site and hurt indexing.
- **Inserting phrases verbatim and awkwardly.** Search engines understand meaning; readability beats exact match.
- **Ignoring zero-volume queries.** Tools miss many rare phrases, yet people still search them.

## FAQ

### Should a new site start with long-tail keywords?

Usually yes: competition is lower, results come sooner, and these pages gradually help the site compete for broader queries.

### How many long-tail queries can one page cover?

As many as share one intent and similar search results. If answering needs a different format or goal, create a separate page.

### Are low-volume and long the same thing?

Almost always, but not always: some short queries are rare and some long ones are popular. Focus on how specific the need is, not the word count.
