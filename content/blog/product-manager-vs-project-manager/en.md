## The short answer

A **Product Manager** answers **"what are we building and why"**. Their job is to make sure the product solves real user problems and delivers business results.

A **Project Manager** answers **"how and when will we get it done"**. Their job is to bring a specific project to completion within the agreed time, budget and scope.

Put simply: the product manager chooses which mountain to climb, the project manager organizes the expedition so it reaches the summit.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Aspect | Product manager | Project manager |
|---|---|---|
| Core question | what to build and why | how and when to deliver |
| Time horizon | a product lives for years; the work never "ends" | a project has a start and an end |
| Success metrics | retention, conversion, revenue, user satisfaction | on time, on budget, in scope, at quality |
| Key artifact | vision, roadmap, prioritized backlog | project plan, schedule, risk register |
| Main relationships | users, business, development team | team, client, vendors |
| Main risk | building something nobody needs | building the right thing late, over budget or incomplete |

## What a product manager does

- **Studies users and the market**: interviews, analytics, competitors, feedback.
- **Frames problems and hypotheses**: what blocks users and how solving it could move metrics.
- **Prioritizes**: decides what to do now, later or never. This is arguably the hardest part of the job.
- **Writes requirements** so the team understands which problem is being solved and how success will be measured.
- **Validates results**: runs experiments, reviews data after release, adjusts course.

Product managers usually do not manage the team directly, so they need to **influence without formal authority**, using arguments and data.

## What a project manager does

- **Plans**: breaks work into phases, estimates timelines with the team, maps dependencies.
- **Manages resources and budget**: who is busy when, where people are missing.
- **Tracks progress**: statuses, blockers, deviations from the plan.
- **Manages risk**: anticipates what could go wrong and prepares a plan B.
- **Communicates**: keeps the client and team informed and negotiates scope changes.

In IT studios and outsourcing companies, the project manager is often the client's main point of contact: agreements, priorities and changes flow through them.

## Skills: shared and different

**Shared:** communication, structured thinking, understanding of the development process, working with Jira or similar trackers, Agile practices (Scrum, Kanban).

**Product-specific:** analytics and metrics, user research, prioritization, understanding the business model.

**Project-specific:** planning and estimation, risk management, budgeting, stakeholder management, delivering under constraints.

## How people get into each role

**Product management** often attracts:
- analysts (data, business, product);
- developers and designers more interested in "why" than "how";
- marketing or customer service specialists who know users well.

**Project management** often attracts:
- coordinators, executive assistants, account managers;
- QA engineers and developers with organizational strengths;
- project managers from other industries (construction, marketing, events).

If you enjoy understanding people and data and are comfortable with uncertainty, look at product. If you like bringing order, planning and finishing things, look at project.

## A common source of confusion

In small companies one position may combine both roles, and job titles do not always match the actual work. Before applying, **read the responsibilities**, not just the title: if the text is mostly about deadlines and reporting, it is a project role; if it is about hypotheses and product metrics, it is a product role.

## FAQ

### Which role is more senior?

Neither. They are different specializations, each with its own levels from junior to head of department.

### Can one person do both?

Yes, especially in startups and small teams. As the product grows, it usually pays to separate them because they need different focus and skills.

### Do I need a technical background?

Both roles benefit from understanding how software is built, but neither requires writing code. For product managers working on technical products, deeper technical knowledge becomes a clear advantage.
