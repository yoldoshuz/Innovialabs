## Why the kickoff matters

A **kickoff** is the first working meeting between the client and the development team after the contract is signed. Its job is to make sure everyone shares the same picture of **what we build, why, for whom and how we will work**. Mistakes missed at this stage cost the most later: a team can spend weeks building the wrong thing.

Good preparation on the client side cuts down on clarifications and rework in the first weeks.

## What to define in advance

### The business goal

Describe in one or two sentences **what problem the project solves** and how you will know it succeeded. For example: "reduce manual processing of requests" or "accept online orders without calling a manager". The goal matters more than the feature list — the team will use it to make small decisions.

### Users

Who will use the product: customers, employees, partners? What do they do today and what gets in their way? If you have real customer complaints or questions, bring examples.

### Constraints

- the desired launch date and why that date;
- the budget range;
- required integrations: CRM, accounting software, payment systems;
- data storage and security requirements.

## Materials to collect

| Material | Why the team needs it |
|---|---|
| Specification or brief | The basis for discussing scope |
| Brand book, logos, fonts | So design starts in the right direction |
| Sites and apps you like and dislike | To understand expectations quickly |
| Access to current systems or a description of them | To assess integrations |
| Texts, photos, product catalog | To understand content volume and who prepares it |
| A map of the current process | To see exactly what is being automated |

It is fine if something is missing. What matters is saying honestly **what exists and what still needs to be prepared**, and who owns it.

## Who to invite

- **The decision-maker** on scope, budget and timeline. Without them the meeting becomes a wish list with no outcome.
- **The product owner** — the person who will be in daily contact with the team.
- **Process experts**: a salesperson, an accountant, an operator — people who know how the work is really done.
- **The IT lead**, if the company has its own systems and servers.

A small group with authority beats a large group without it.

## Questions to ask the contractor

- How will communication work: channels, call frequency, who is the manager?
- How often will you show results?
- How are requirement changes handled?
- Which reports will we receive?
- What do you need from us, and by when?
- Who owns the code, domains and service accounts?
- How do testing and acceptance work?

## What you should leave with

After the meeting both sides should have a **short summary document**:

- the project goal and success criteria;
- the agreed scope of the first phase and what is excluded;
- contacts and roles;
- the meeting schedule and reporting format;
- the client's task list with dates;
- open questions and who closes them.

If nobody writes this down, ask the contractor to send it in writing.

## Common mistakes

- Coming "to listen" without decisions on the key questions.
- Not inviting the person who signs off.
- Hiding budget or deadline limits — the team will plan something you cannot accept.
- Not assigning an owner for content: it is a frequent reason launches slip.

## FAQ

### How long does a kickoff meeting take?

Usually from an hour to half a working day, depending on project size. Large projects sometimes split it into several themed sessions instead of one long meeting.

### What if the specification is not ready yet?

That is normal. Bring the business goal, a description of users and the constraints. The contractor can propose a separate discovery phase where requirements are gathered together.

### Should the company's CEO attend?

If they make the final calls on budget and scope — yes, at least for part of the meeting. Otherwise agreements will have to be renegotiated.
