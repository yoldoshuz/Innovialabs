## In short: the reports you need

To keep control of a project without digging into the code, five kinds of reports are enough:

1. **Progress report** — what is done, what is in progress, what comes next.
2. **Burndown** — how much work is left and whether the team is on track.
3. **Budget burn** — how much has been spent compared with the plan and with what is done.
4. **Risk register** — what could go wrong and what is being done about it.
5. **Time logs** — where exactly the hours went.

Agree on the set and frequency of reports at the start and record them in the contract or project plan.

## Progress report

Usually weekly or at the end of each sprint. A good report contains:

- completed tasks linked to features you can check;
- tasks in progress and expected dates;
- blockers, especially those that depend on you: access, texts, decisions;
- the plan for the next period.

**How to read it:** the key question is whether you can **see the result**. A report without a demo or a link to a test version is a reason to ask to see the work live. A warning sign is the same tasks sitting "in progress" for weeks.

## Burndown

A **burndown chart** plots time on one axis and remaining work on the other. The ideal line goes down to zero by the end of the sprint or phase.

**How to read it:**

| What you see | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| The line stays close to the ideal | The team is on schedule |
| The line is flat | Work is not being closed or tasks are too large |
| The line goes up | New tasks are being added to the scope |
| A sharp drop at the end | Tasks are closed in a batch, possibly without proper checks |

A rising line is a frequent sign of **scope creep**. Ask who added the tasks and why.

## Budget burn

For time-and-materials projects this is the key report. It compares three values:

- the **planned budget** as of today;
- the **actual spend**;
- the **value of completed work** — what the finished part should have cost according to plan.

**How to read it:** if a lot has been spent but little completed, the project is getting more expensive. Spending less than planned is not always good news: the team may simply be slower, and the delay will show up later. Look at the **trend over several periods**, not at a single point.

## Risk register

A simple table: risk, likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner. For example: "the payment provider's API is unavailable in test mode", "a key developer is on vacation".

**How to read it:** be suspicious if the register is **always empty** or has not changed for months — every project has risks. It is a good sign when risks get closed or turn into tasks with a clear solution.

## Time logs

A breakdown of hours by task and specialist. Especially important with hourly billing.

**How to read it:**

- are there tasks that took much longer than estimated;
- do many hours go to vague categories like "meetings" or "other";
- does the distribution match the plan — for example, testing should not disappear from the logs.

The goal is not to catch an extra minute but to understand **where the project loses time**.

## How the reports connect

The most useful conclusions come from comparing them:

- burndown is flat but hours are being logged — work happens but does not finish;
- progress looks good but budget burns faster — estimates were too low;
- no risks are listed but deadlines slip — risks are not being recorded.

When you see a mismatch, ask for a short call and a recovery plan, not just an explanation.

## FAQ

### How often should I request reports?

Progress reports weekly or per sprint. Budget burn and time logs weekly or monthly, depending on project size. The risk register whenever something significant changes.

### What if my contractor does not produce such reports?

Ask for at least a simple format: a table of completed tasks and hours spent, plus a regular demo. Most task trackers can generate these reports automatically.

### Do I need access to the task tracker?

It is a good idea. Read access to the tracker gives you a current picture without waiting for a report and makes it easier to discuss specific tasks.
