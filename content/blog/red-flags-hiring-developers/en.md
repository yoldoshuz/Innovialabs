## In short: what to watch for

An unreliable vendor is rarely given away by a single detail. It is usually a combination: promises without questions, reluctance to put things in writing, and control over your code. Below are the main signals and what to do instead of agreeing.

## 1. An unrealistic quote

**Signal:** the price is far below the others, or the quote comes instantly without a single clarifying question.

**Why it is bad:** the vendor either did not understand the task or plans to make up the budget through "extra work".

**What to do:** ask for a breakdown by feature and a list of assumptions. Compare what exactly each candidate includes.

## 2. No written scope

**Signal:** "Let's just start and figure it out as we go", a contract with no feature appendix.

**Why it is bad:** at the end of the project there is nothing to prove what was promised.

**What to do:** document the scope and acceptance criteria, at least for the first stage. Changes go through written amendments.

## 3. Refusing code access

**Signal:** the code lives only with the vendor, access comes "after full payment" or "is not allowed".

**Why it is bad:** you cannot check progress or quality, and switching vendors becomes nearly impossible.

**What to do:** agree that the repository is created on your account or that you get access from day one. Code ownership goes in the contract.

## 4. Vague timelines

**Signal:** "A couple of months or so", with no stages or intermediate results.

**Why it is bad:** you cannot tell whether the project is on track until it is too late.

**What to do:** split the project into stages, each with a concrete deliverable and a demo date.

## 5. Prepayment for the whole project

**Signal:** a demand to pay everything or most of it upfront.

**Why it is bad:** the vendor loses the incentive to keep deadlines and quality.

**What to do:** pay by stages, tied to accepted results.

## 6. Accounts registered to the vendor

**Signal:** the domain, server, App Store and Google Play accounts and payment systems are registered in the vendor's name.

**Why it is bad:** your business depends on someone else's accounts.

**What to do:** all key accounts belong to your company; the vendor gets access as a member.

## 7. No questions for you

**Signal:** the vendor agrees with everything and clarifies nothing.

**Why it is bad:** a good team always asks about users, scenarios and constraints — you cannot build a quality product without that.

**What to do:** notice which candidates suggest alternatives and point out risks.

## 8. No way to talk to past clients

**Signal:** there is a portfolio, but client contacts are "confidential" and projects cannot be opened.

**What to do:** ask for at least one live project you can check, or a conversation with a client.

## 9. Silence about testing and support

**Signal:** "How do you test?" gets a vague answer; post-launch support is never discussed.

**What to do:** put a warranty period and support terms in the contract.

## Summary table

| Signal | Defense |
|---|---|
| Price with no breakdown | Feature breakdown and assumptions |
| No scope | Written spec and acceptance criteria |
| Code only with the vendor | Your repository from day one |
| Vague timelines | Stages with demos |
| Full prepayment | Payment for accepted stages |
| Accounts in vendor's name | Accounts in your company's name |

## FAQ

### If there is only one red flag, should I walk away?

Not necessarily. Discuss it openly: a reliable vendor is usually willing to adjust terms, for example by giving repository access or splitting payment into stages. A refusal to discuss it is the real warning.

### What if problems appear mid-project?

Request access to the code and the current state of the project, put agreements in writing, and ask an independent specialist to review code quality before making a decision.

### Does a contract fully protect me?

A contract reduces risk but does not replace verification. Regular demos, code access and staged payments give you control long before things reach the point of claims.
