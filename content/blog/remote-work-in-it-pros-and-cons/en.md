## The short answer: who remote work suits

Remote work in IT suits people who **plan their own day**, write clearly and are comfortable asking questions in chat. It suits people at the very start of their career less well: beginners need a lot of fast feedback, and remotely that feedback has to be actively sought out.

Remote is neither better nor worse than the office. It is a different way of working with its own costs, and it helps to know them in advance.

## The upsides

- **Deep focus.** No open-space noise or random interruptions, so it is easier to block long stretches of time for hard problems.
- **No commute.** Those hours can go to sleep, exercise or learning.
- **A bigger job market.** You can work for companies in other cities and countries without relocating.
- **Flexible hours.** Many teams care about results rather than time spent online.

## The downsides people mention less

### Communication overhead

In an office, a question is solved in a minute at a colleague's desk. Remotely, the same question becomes a message, a wait and sometimes a call. Teams have to write more: documentation, task descriptions, meeting notes. That is useful, but it takes time.

### Career visibility

Your manager does not see you helping teammates or untangling a tricky problem. If you do not talk about it, your contribution is easy to underestimate during salary reviews or promotions. **Visibility in remote work has to be created on purpose.**

### Isolation

Without hallway chats and shared lunches, people tend to feel lonely over time. The line between work and home blurs: the laptop is always nearby, and the workday quietly stretches.

## Why remote is harder for juniors

Beginners learn not only from tasks but from observation: how senior colleagues discuss architecture, run code reviews and handle incidents. Remotely, that background learning disappears.

Typical struggles:

- **Fear of "bothering" people**, which leads to spending hours stuck on one problem.
- **Slow feedback**: a mistake shows up only in review, not at the moment it could have been fixed quickly.
- **Unclear expectations**: it is hard to tell whether your pace is normal.

If you are a junior choosing between office, hybrid and remote with everything else equal, an office or hybrid setup in your first year often leads to faster growth.

## How to make remote work actually work

| Problem | What helps |
|---|---|
| Slow replies | Ask with context: what you tried, what you expected, what happened |
| Invisible contribution | A short weekly update: done, blocked, next |
| Getting stuck | A rule: stuck for more than an hour, ask |
| Blurred boundaries | Fixed start and end times, a dedicated workspace |
| Isolation | Regular 1:1s, pair programming, in-person meetups |

A few more habits:

- **Turn your camera on** for important discussions; it builds trust faster.
- **Post call summaries** in a shared channel so decisions do not get lost.
- **Agree on overlap hours** if the team spans time zones.
- **Look after your health**: a decent chair, breaks and movement during the day.

## How to tell if it is for you

Ask yourself:

1. Can I start work without outside pressure and finish on time?
2. Am I comfortable explaining a problem in writing?
3. Do I have a quiet place to work?
4. Do I get enough social contact outside work?

If most answers are "yes", remote work will probably suit you. If not, start with a hybrid schedule and gradually increase the number of remote days.

## FAQ

### Can I get my first IT job as a remote position?

Yes, but competition for such roles is higher and onboarding is harder. If you have a choice, a first year in an office or hybrid team usually helps you gain experience faster and learn how teamwork really runs.

### How do I avoid burnout when working remotely?

Keep clear boundaries for the workday, take breaks and avoid answering work messages late at night unless it is urgent. Regular contact with colleagues and a life away from the screen lower the risk.

### Does remote work affect salary growth?

Not directly, but if your contribution is invisible it is harder to evaluate. Share your results regularly, take part in discussions and pick up work that the team can see.
