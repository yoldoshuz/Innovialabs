## The core principle

Choose a model where **customers pay more when they get more value**. To do that, define your **value metric** — something that grows along with the customer's benefit: number of employees, processed orders, messages sent, projects.

The five main models:

| Model | How customers pay | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Flat | one price for everything | the product is simple and customers are similar |
| Tiered | several packages of features and limits | customers differ in size |
| Per-seat | for each user in the account | value grows with team size |
| Usage-based | by volume: requests, transactions, gigabytes | usage varies greatly |
| Freemium | basic version free, advanced version paid | the product spreads through users |

## Flat pricing

One subscription, all features.

- **Pros:** easy to explain and sell, predictable revenue.
- **Cons:** small customers overpay, large ones underpay; no path to grow the deal size.
- **Example:** a narrow single-purpose tool, such as an online booking service for small salons.

## Tiered pricing

Usually three or four packages that differ in features and limits.

- **Pros:** covers different segments and gives a natural upgrade path.
- **Cons:** easy to overcomplicate; hard to choose if the differences are unclear.
- **Example:** a CRM with plans for a small business, a growing company and an enterprise.

Tip: make the differences between tiers clear and tied to real needs, not a random set of features.

## Per-seat pricing

The price is multiplied by the number of users with access.

- **Pros:** revenue grows with the customer; a familiar model in B2B.
- **Cons:** customers save on seats and share one account; the product spreads more slowly inside a company.
- **Example:** task trackers, team messengers, sales team tools.

## Usage-based pricing

Customers pay for actual volume: API requests, SMS sent, transactions, storage.

- **Pros:** low entry barrier; the price fairly reflects load and your costs.
- **Cons:** revenue is harder to forecast; customers fear surprise bills.
- **Example:** cloud services, payment gateways, AI APIs, email services.

To ease customers' fears, add limits, spending alerts and a cost calculator.

## Freemium

A free version with limits, a paid one without them.

- **Pros:** fast user growth; the product sells itself.
- **Cons:** free users cost money (infrastructure, support); most of them may never pay.
- **Example:** products whose value is obvious right away and where users invite colleagues — note apps, design tools, file storage.

Freemium is more a marketing strategy than a pricing model. It is often combined with tiers.

## Hybrid models

In practice, models are combined:

- **tier + per seat**: a feature package priced by the number of seats;
- **subscription + usage**: the base fee includes a quota, anything above it is billed by volume;
- **free plan + paid tiers**.

## How to choose

1. Define the customer's value metric.
2. Check that customers understand what they pay for and can predict their bill.
3. Compare the price with your cost of serving the customer.
4. Look at how people pay for alternatives in your market.
5. Test the price on early customers and be ready to change it.

**Common mistakes:** pricing too low "to attract customers", too many tiers, a metric the customer cannot control, and not revisiting pricing for years.

## FAQ

### Can I change the pricing model after launch?

Yes, and that is normal. Existing customers often keep their current terms for a while, and the new model applies to new customers — this way you keep their trust.

### How many tiers should I start with?

Two or three are enough. More tiers make the choice harder and slow down sales while you still know little about your customers.

### Which is better for B2B — per-seat or usage-based?

It depends on where the value is. If the product helps every employee, per-seat fits. If the benefit depends on the volume of operations, usage-based is fairer. A combination of both often works.
