## The short answer: how to ask for more

Salary negotiation is a normal part of hiring, and companies expect it. A strong position rests on three things: **market data**, **clear numbers of your own** and **calm arguments** based on the value you bring. "I want more" without reasons rarely works. A request grounded in the market, your experience and the role's scope is an ordinary business conversation.

## Step 1. Research the market

- **Job postings with listed salary ranges** on job boards and Telegram channels, for your role, level and stack.
- **Public salary reports** from job boards and professional communities. Check the methodology and the date.
- **Conversations with peers** and recruiters you know. Ask about ranges, not exact figures for specific people.
- **Account for context**: city or remote, currency, company size, foreign or local employer, gross or net.

The result is not one number but **a range** for your position.

## Step 2. Set your numbers

Before the conversation, decide for yourself:

- **Your minimum**: below this you walk away. Base it on your expenses and your alternatives.
- **Your target**: a realistic market figure given your experience.
- **Your opening number**: slightly above the target, leaving room to negotiate.

Prepare **arguments**: relevant experience, results, rare skills and how closely your profile matches the role.

## Step 3. When to name a number

- If the posting lists a range, use it and explain where in that range you fit.
- If you are asked for expectations early, you can **give a market-based range** or politely suggest returning to numbers once the scope and level are clear.
- The best moment for serious negotiation is **after the company has decided to hire you** but before you sign. That is when you have the most leverage.
- Name **a range whose lower end you are happy with**: the other side often anchors on it.

## Step 4. Handling a counteroffer

1. **Do not answer immediately.** Thank them and ask for time to think. That is normal.
2. **Clarify the full offer**: bonuses, review schedule, probation, taxes, type of contract.
3. **Respond with arguments**: "Given my experience with X and the scope of Y, I am looking for Z. Can we get closer to that?"
4. **Use other offers honestly.** If you have them, you can mention it without pressure. Never invent alternatives.
5. **If your current employer counters**, think about why you started looking. A raise does not always fix the original reason.

## Step 5. What to negotiate beyond salary

When the salary budget is fixed, there is often flexibility elsewhere:

| Term | What to discuss |
|---|---|
| Review | Date and criteria for a salary review, for example after probation |
| Schedule | Remote, hybrid, flexible hours |
| Learning | Courses, conferences, certifications paid by the company |
| Time off | Extra vacation days |
| Equipment and expenses | Laptop, internet and home office costs |
| Role | Scope, level, growth path |

**Put important agreements in writing**, in the offer or at least in an email.

## Common mistakes

- Going into the conversation without market data.
- Opening with your minimum.
- Accepting on the spot without time to think.
- Justifying the request with personal expenses instead of value to the company.
- Bluffing about offers that do not exist.
- Forgetting to check whether the figure is gross or net.

## FAQ

### Will they withdraw the offer if I ask for more?

A polite, reasoned request is a normal part of hiring, and most companies are prepared for it. The risk appears with ultimatums, a harsh tone or figures clearly disconnected from the market.

### How do I ask for a raise at my current job?

List your results and how your responsibilities have grown, compare your pay with the market and ask your manager for a dedicated meeting. If a raise is not possible now, agree on criteria and a date for the next conversation.

### Should I disclose my current salary?

You do not have to. You can say you are guided by the market and the scope of the new role, and share your expected range. This keeps the discussion on the future position, not the past one.
