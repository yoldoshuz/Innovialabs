## Scrum in a nutshell

**Scrum** is a popular framework for building products according to Agile principles. Work happens in **sprints** — short, fixed-length cycles, usually one to four weeks. At the end of every sprint the team shows a finished, working part of the product.

Scrum defines three things: **artifacts** (what we plan), **roles** (who is responsible for what) and **events** (when and why we meet). Below is everything a business owner needs to know.

## Artifacts: the foundation

- **Product Backlog** — one list of everything the product may need: features, improvements, fixes. Sorted by value, with the most important on top.
- **Sprint Backlog** — the tasks the team took into the current sprint, plus the plan to deliver them.
- **Increment** — the sprint's result: a working version of the product with new features that can be shown or released.

A key concept is the **Definition of Done**: a shared understanding of when a task is truly finished (for example: code reviewed, tested, deployed to a staging server).

## Roles: who does what

| Role | What they do | Who it is for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Product Owner** | Decides what to build and in which order; manages the backlog; owns product value | Someone from the business side or a trusted representative |
| **Scrum Master** | Keeps the process working, removes blockers, helps both team and client | Usually on the development team's side |
| **Developers** | Engineers, designers, testers — everyone who builds the product | The contractor's team or your own |

The main takeaway for a business owner: **the Product Owner is the key role, and it is best kept on your side**. If only the contractor sets priorities, the product will evolve according to their view, not yours.

## Events: which meetings exist

| Meeting | When | Purpose | Business owner needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sprint Planning** | Start of the sprint | Choose the sprint goal and tasks | Product Owner — required; owner — optional |
| **Daily Scrum** | Every day, briefly | Team synchronization | No |
| **Sprint Review** | End of the sprint | Show results, gather feedback | **Yes, this is your key meeting** |
| **Retrospective** | After the review | Improve how the team works | Usually no |

### Sprint Review is your main checkpoint

At the sprint review you see a **live product**, not a slide deck. This is where you should:

- check whether the work solves a real business problem;
- say what to change or remove;
- discuss what to do in the next sprint.

### Daily Scrum is not for you

The daily meeting is the team's internal tool. When a client executive attends, it often turns into a status report "for the boss" and loses its value.

## What one sprint looks like

1. **Planning:** the Product Owner explains priorities, the team picks what it can finish and formulates the sprint goal.
2. **Work:** the team builds the tasks and syncs daily. The sprint content does not change mid-way unless absolutely necessary.
3. **Review:** the team shows the result, you give feedback, the backlog is updated.
4. **Retrospective:** the team discusses what to improve in the process.
5. The next sprint starts right away.

## Common mistakes on the business side

- **No Product Owner of your own** — anyone sets priorities and decisions drag on.
- **Adding tasks mid-sprint** — the team loses focus and the sprint goal fails.
- **Skipping the Sprint Review** — feedback arrives too late.
- **Asking for reports instead of demos** — you see percentages, not the product.
- **Using Scrum as a formality**: meetings happen, but nobody revisits priorities.

## FAQ

### How long should a sprint be?

The official Scrum Guide caps a sprint at one month. In practice many teams choose one or two weeks: feedback comes more often and the plan is easier to adjust.

### Can the business owner be the Product Owner?

Yes, if they have time to work with the backlog regularly and answer the team. If not, it is better to appoint a trusted employee with the authority to make decisions.

### Does Scrum suit small projects?

For very small or one-off tasks, full Scrum can be overkill. But its core — short cycles, a shared backlog and regular demos — is useful in almost any project.
