## The short answer

An article reaches the top not because of keyword count but because it **answers the query better than the alternatives**. The workflow: study the search results, understand intent, build an outline with subtopics, write for a human, then polish the on-page elements. Skipping the first step is the most common reason a good text never ranks.

## Step 1. Analyze the SERP

Search your main query in Google and Yandex and review the top 10 results. Answer these questions:

- **What is the intent?** Informational (how to), commercial (buy, price) or comparative (which is better).
- **Which format wins?** Guide, list, comparison, glossary.
- **Which subtopics repeat** across most leaders — that is your required minimum.
- **What is missing?** Outdated data, no examples, an unanswered common question — that is your opening.

If the top results are all product pages, an informational article will not get there, no matter how well it is written.

## Step 2. Outline and heading structure

Build the outline before writing:

1. One **H1** — close to the title, containing the main query.
2. **H2s** — the key subtopics from SERP analysis and user questions.
3. **H3s** — details inside subtopics, steps, options.

A good H2 makes sense on its own: "Delivery costs" beats "Nuances". Readers and search engines should grasp the article from the headings alone.

## Step 3. Keywords without stuffing

Collect your semantic core: the main query, 3–10 close variations and related terms. Then:

- put the main query in the title, H1, first paragraph and URL;
- use variations and modifiers in H2s and the body where they sound natural;
- **do not repeat** the same phrase in every paragraph — search engines recognize stuffing and demote such pages.

A simple test: read the text aloud. If a sentence sounds robotic, rewrite it.

## Step 4. Cover the subtopics

Completeness matters more than length. The article should answer **every question the reader will have while doing the task**: what it is, how to do it, how long it takes, common mistakes, how to choose. Where to find those questions:

- "People also ask" blocks in search results;
- search bar autocomplete;
- questions your sales and support teams hear.

The first paragraph gives the answer right away — do not make people read a preamble.

## Step 5. On-page finishing

| Element | What to check |
|---|---|
| Title | Up to ~60 characters, query near the start |
| Description | 120–160 characters, benefit and call to action |
| URL | Short, latin, includes the keyword |
| Images | Compressed, with meaningful alt text |
| Internal links | 2–5 links to related pages |
| Readability | Short paragraphs, lists, tables |

Add structured data (for example FAQ or Article) where appropriate, and make sure the page loads fast on mobile.

## Common mistakes

- Writing before analyzing the SERP and missing the intent.
- Padding the text with filler to hit a word count.
- Copying a competitor's structure without adding value.
- Forgetting internal links, leaving the article orphaned.
- Never updating published content.

## FAQ

### How long should an SEO article be?

There is no fixed rule. Look at the top results and at completeness: if a topic can be covered briefly and clearly, a longer text gives no advantage.

### How many keywords should I use?

Focus on naturalness, not count. Put the main query in key places and use synonyms and related terms wherever they fit the meaning.

### When will I see results?

It depends on competition, site authority and indexing speed. Results are usually assessed over weeks or months, and articles are refined over time using Search Console and Yandex Webmaster data.
