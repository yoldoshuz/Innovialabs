## The short answer

**Sans serif** fonts are the default for interfaces: buttons, forms, navigation, dashboards and small labels. **Serif** fonts work well for headings with personality and for long-form reading such as articles and editorial pages. On today's high-resolution screens, readability depends far more on size, spacing and the quality of the specific font than on whether it has serifs.

So the real question is not "which is more readable" but "what character does this product need, and where will the text appear".

## Main font classifications

| Class | Recognizable by | Typical character | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serif** | Small strokes at the ends of letters | Classic, editorial, trustworthy | Articles, books, premium and editorial brands |
| **Sans serif** | No serifs, even strokes | Neutral, modern, clean | Interfaces, apps, body text on sites |
| **Slab serif** | Thick, block-like serifs | Sturdy, confident, a bit technical | Headings, posters, bold brands |
| **Monospace** | Every character has the same width | Technical, precise | Code, tables of numbers, technical accents |
| **Display / decorative** | Expressive, unusual shapes | Strong personality | Logos and large headings only |
| **Script / handwritten** | Imitates handwriting | Personal, informal | Rare accents, never body text |

Within sans serif there are useful subgroups: **grotesque and neo-grotesque** (neutral), **geometric** (built on circles and straight lines, friendly and modern) and **humanist** (closer to handwriting, very readable at small sizes).

## How they behave on screens

The old rule "serifs are bad on screens" came from low-resolution displays, where thin serifs turned into noisy pixels. On modern displays good serif fonts render cleanly at body sizes.

What still matters:

- **Small sizes.** At 12–14px, sans serif fonts with open shapes and a generous x-height stay clearer. That is why UI labels are almost always sans serif.
- **Thin details.** Serif fonts with very high stroke contrast (hairline strokes) look elegant in large headings but weaken at small sizes and in light weights.
- **Similar letters.** In interfaces with codes, IDs and passwords, check that I, l and 1, or O and 0, are easy to tell apart.
- **Language support.** For multilingual products confirm the font has full Cyrillic and Latin extended characters, including the letters your languages need. Missing glyphs fall back to another font and break the look.

## Pairing fonts

A safe structure for most projects:

1. **One family for everything** — a sans serif with several weights. Simplest and most consistent.
2. **Serif headings + sans serif body** — adds character and editorial feel while keeping UI text clear.
3. **Sans serif headings + serif body** — good for blogs and long reading.
4. **Add monospace** only if you show code or technical data.

Rules that keep a pair working:

- Pair fonts with **clear contrast** in structure, not two almost identical sans serifs.
- Match **x-height and proportions** roughly so they sit well on the same line.
- Limit yourself to **two families**; use weights and sizes for further variety.
- Check both fonts in every language and every weight you plan to use.

## How to choose for your project

- **Product interface or SaaS dashboard:** sans serif, possibly one family only.
- **Corporate or service website:** sans serif body, headings either sans or serif depending on brand tone.
- **Media, blog, documentation:** serif or humanist sans for body, comfortable line length.
- **Premium, legal, finance, heritage brands:** serif headings often support the tone.
- **Tech and developer products:** sans serif plus monospace accents.

Also consider practical factors: license terms for web and apps, file size and the number of weights you load, and variable font versions that can replace several files.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a font by its name or a single word in a preview instead of a full paragraph.
- Using a decorative or script font for body text or buttons.
- Mixing three or more families on one page.
- Ignoring Cyrillic or other scripts until translations arrive.
- Loading many weights that the design never uses.

## FAQ

### Are serif fonts harder to read on screens?

Not on modern displays at normal reading sizes. Differences appear mainly at very small sizes and with high-contrast hairline serifs, where a sans serif usually stays clearer.

### Can I use a serif font in a mobile app?

Yes, especially for headings or reading-focused screens. For small UI elements such as tab labels and input hints, a sans serif is usually the more reliable choice.

### What is the safest font choice for a new product?

A well-made sans serif family with good language support and several weights. It works across interface and content, and you can add a second font later if the brand needs more character.
