## What the stages are

The **Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)** is the path a product takes from idea to a working service. In most projects it looks like this:

1. Discovery — research and planning.
2. Design — prototypes and interfaces.
3. Development — writing the code.
4. Testing — quality checks.
5. Release — launch for users.
6. Support — maintenance and growth.

Stages can run one after another (the waterfall model) or repeat in short cycles (Agile). Their content stays the same either way.

## 1. Discovery

The team digs into the business problem: who the users are, what their pain points are, what already exists on the market, and what the time and budget constraints look like.

**What you get:** a description of goals, a prioritized feature list, a user flow map, a preliminary architecture and an estimate of time and cost.

## 2. Design

First come **wireframes** — screen layouts without visual styling. Then the final **UI design** and a clickable prototype.

**What you get:** mockups of all key screens, a prototype you can "walk through", and a UI kit — a set of components for development.

## 3. Development

Developers build the frontend and backend, set up the database and integrations. In Agile, work is split into **sprints** — short iterations, each ending with a demo of the result.

**What you get:** working parts of the product on a staging server, regular demos and access to the task tracker.

## 4. Testing

QA engineers verify that everything works according to requirements: features, different devices and browsers, load and security. Some checks are automated.

**What you get:** reports on bugs found and fixed, and a stable version ready for acceptance.

## 5. Release

The product is deployed to production servers, published to the App Store and Google Play, and connected to a domain, analytics and monitoring.

**What you get:** a working product for real users, access to all services and documentation.

## 6. Support and growth

After launch come user feedback, new ideas and the inevitable minor bugs. Dependencies need updates, servers need watching and features need adding.

**What you get:** stable operation, fixes and a development plan based on real data.

## Your role at each stage

| Stage | Your role |
|---|---|
| Discovery | Share knowledge about the business and users |
| Design | Approve user flows and mockups |
| Development | Watch demos, set priorities |
| Testing | Run acceptance |
| Release | Provide access, accounts and legal details |
| Support | Collect feedback, plan next steps |

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping discovery.** Misunderstanding the task costs the most to fix later.
- **Cutting testing to hit a deadline.** Then users find the bugs instead of the team.
- **Not planning for support.** A product without maintenance degrades over time.
- **Not taking ownership of access.** Code, domains and accounts should belong to you.

## FAQ

### Can a stage be skipped?

Formally yes, but the risks do not disappear. Without a dedicated design stage, for example, developers invent the interface on the fly, which is usually slower and worse.

### How is Agile different from waterfall?

In waterfall, stages follow strictly one after another. In Agile, the product is built in short cycles, each containing a bit of design, development and testing.

### When does support start?

Right after release. It is best to agree on it in advance so you are not left without a team in the product's first weeks.
