## The ROI formula

**ROI (return on investment)** shows how much you get back for every unit of money invested:

```text
ROI = (Benefit − Cost) / Cost × 100%
```

Both values are calculated **over the same period** — usually 2-3 years, because software rarely pays off immediately. The second key metric is the **payback period**: how many months it takes for the accumulated benefit to equal the investment.

## Step 1. Count the full cost

A common mistake is counting only development. Calculate the **TCO (total cost of ownership)**:

- development: analysis, design, coding, testing;
- infrastructure: servers, cloud, domains, third-party APIs;
- licenses and subscriptions for services you use;
- support and improvements after launch;
- rollout: staff training, data migration;
- your own team's time spent on requirements and acceptance.

## Step 2. Estimate the benefit

Benefits come in two kinds.

**Savings:**

- staff hours freed by automation (hours × hourly cost);
- fewer errors and the losses they cause;
- retiring old systems and subscriptions.

**Revenue growth:**

- new sales through a new channel (website, bot, app);
- higher conversion or average order value;
- more repeat purchases thanks to CRM and messaging.

Estimate **conservatively** and base it on your current numbers: how many leads get lost, how much time goes into routine work. Write down every assumption — you will need to revisit them.

## Step 3. A worked example

A hypothetical example in abstract units (u) — a company introduces a CRM that automates lead handling. Horizon: 3 years.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Development and rollout (one-off) | 100 u |
| Support and infrastructure | 20 u per year |
| Managers' time saved | 60 u per year |
| Extra profit from fewer lost leads | 20 u per year |

Cost over 3 years: 100 + 20 × 3 = **160 u**
Benefit over 3 years: (60 + 20) × 3 = **240 u**

ROI = (240 − 160) / 160 × 100% = **50%**

Note: count **profit** from sales as the benefit, not total revenue — otherwise ROI will be inflated.

## Step 4. Payback period

Net benefit per month: (80 − 20) / 12 = 5 u.
Payback period: 100 / 5 = **20 months**.

Keep in mind that benefits rarely start on day one: during development and rollout they are zero. So the real payback period, counted from the start of the project, will be longer.

## Making the estimate more reliable

- **Calculate three scenarios** — pessimistic, base and optimistic.
- **Start with an MVP**: test the key hypothesis cheaper and refine the numbers with real data.
- **Record metrics before launch** so you have something to compare against.
- **Track intangible effects** separately: customer convenience, data transparency, less dependence on specific people. They are hard to convert into money but still affect the decision.

## Common mistakes

- Counting development and forgetting support.
- Using revenue instead of profit.
- Choosing too short a horizon — software rarely pays off within a few months.
- Never checking the estimate after launch.

## FAQ

### What counts as a good ROI?

There is no universal benchmark. Compare the project's ROI with alternatives: other ways to invest the same money, or the cost of doing nothing while manual processes keep growing.

### What if the benefit is hard to express in money?

Find indirect metrics: lead handling time, error count, share of repeat customers. Convert them into money via hourly cost or average profit per customer, and write down your assumptions.

### Should I account for inflation and the time value of money?

For large, long projects — yes, through discounting (NPV). For smaller projects with a 2-3 year horizon, simple ROI and payback period are usually enough.
