## The short answer

Only people who leave abruptly for a distant field really start from zero. An experienced specialist already has what a newcomer lacks: an understanding of how software gets built, teamwork, product and domain knowledge. The goal is to **move into a neighbouring role** where those skills count, and to **gain experience in the new role before your title changes**.

Be honest with yourself: your new hard skills will likely be weaker than in your current role. You keep your grade when transferable skills make up for that gap.

## Common moves and what carries over

| Move | What carries over | What you need to build |
|---|---|---|
| **QA → development** | Product knowledge, test design, test automation, attention to edge cases | Architecture, algorithms, depth in a language and framework |
| **Backend → DevOps** | Linux, networking, databases, knowing how the application behaves | Infrastructure as code, CI/CD, containers, monitoring, cloud |
| **Developer → PM** | Estimating complexity, understanding technical constraints, working with the team | Requirements and metrics, prioritisation, stakeholder communication |
| **Frontend → backend** | The language (if it is, say, JavaScript/TypeScript), HTTP, APIs, Git | Databases, queues, scaling, security |
| **Developer → data analyst** | SQL, programming, logical thinking | Statistics, visualisation, product thinking |

The more overlap in the middle column, the lower the risk of a grade drop.

## A step-by-step transition plan

1. **Define a concrete goal.** Not "I want DevOps" but "I want to own CI/CD and infrastructure for a service running on Kubernetes".
2. **Map the gap.** Collect requirements from several job postings for the target role and mark what you can already do, what you know partially and what is missing.
3. **Take boundary tasks.** A QA engineer writes automated tests and fixes simple bugs, a backend developer sets up the pipeline for their own service, a developer breaks down a feature and talks to the client. That is experience you can put on your resume.
4. **Agree a plan with your manager.** An internal move is the most reliable route: the company knows your level and you know the product. Propose a plan with specific tasks and a trial period.
5. **Find a mentor in the target role.** Regular reviews from an experienced colleague save months of studying alone.
6. **Rewrite your resume for the new role.** Lead with experience relevant to the goal. Phrase it as outcomes: "set up deployment for a service", not "interested in DevOps".

## How to keep your grade

- **Move internally** if you can: it is easier to agree on keeping your level and salary during the adaptation period.
- **Choose a hybrid position.** For example, SDET instead of a "pure" developer role, or platform engineer on a team that values backend experience.
- **Sell the combination.** A developer with strong QA experience, or a PM who understands architecture, is a distinct asset rather than "a junior in a new role".
- **Be ready to compromise.** Sometimes it is wiser to accept a slightly lower level for a while, with a clear growth plan, than to postpone the move for years.

## Common mistakes

- **Learning in a vacuum.** Courses without real tasks carry little weight with employers. Practice on a work project outweighs a certificate.
- **Quitting with nowhere to go.** Leaving before you have experience in the new role sharply raises the risk of starting over.
- **Downplaying past experience.** In interviews, explain how it helps in the new role instead of apologising for it.
- **Switching because of fatigue.** If the real cause is burnout or a toxic team, a new specialization may not fix it. Figure out what exactly is wrong first.

## FAQ

### How long does a specialization switch take?

It depends on how close the roles are, how many boundary tasks you can take on in your current job and how much time you put into learning. An adjacent move inside your company usually goes noticeably faster than changing direction through a new job search.

### Should I mention my previous role on my resume?

Yes. Hiding experience makes no sense — it is part of your value. Describe it so it is clear which skills carry over to the new role.

### What if my company has no openings in the target role?

Build experience through boundary tasks and side projects, document the results, then go to the market with a resume where the new role is already backed by real work.
