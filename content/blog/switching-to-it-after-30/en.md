## Is it realistic

**Yes.** Age itself is not the barrier: employers care whether you solve problems. Someone over 30 brings what a fresh graduate lacks — experience with people, deadlines, responsibility and knowledge of a real industry.

The difficulties are elsewhere: less free time, more financial obligations and a temporary drop in income. So plan the switch as a project, not an impulse.

## How to use your past experience

The shortest path is a role where your previous field becomes an advantage.

| Past experience | Matching IT roles |
|---|---|
| Sales, customer work | Project/Product manager, presales, customer success |
| Accounting, finance | Business analyst, data analyst, ERP/CRM implementation |
| Teaching | Technical writer, IT course designer, QA |
| Logistics, manufacturing | Systems analyst, automation rollout |
| Design, print | UI/UX designer |
| Engineering, hard sciences | Backend development, data analytics, DevOps |

**Domain expertise** is valued: an analyst who understands accounting finds common ground with a client faster than someone who only knows SQL.

## Roles that suit career changers

- **QA (testing).** A clear entry point; attention to detail and structure matter.
- **Business and systems analysis.** Requires logic, good questions and experience talking to clients.
- **Project management.** Fits people-management experience, but you need to understand how development works.
- **Data analytics.** SQL and spreadsheets plus domain knowledge.
- **Development.** The longest learning path, but the widest job market.

## Planning money and time

### Money

- Calculate your fixed monthly expenses and build a **cushion** for the study and job-search period.
- Expect that your first IT position may pay less than your current income.
- If you have no cushion, do not quit: study alongside your job.

### Time

- Consistency beats volume: steady weekly sessions work better than rare marathons.
- Block study time in your calendar like work meetings.
- Agree with your family on a period when you will be less available.

### Step-by-step plan

1. Choose a role using the table above and your interests.
2. Find 2–3 job postings for that role and list the requirements — that is your curriculum.
3. Study and build projects using material from your previous field.
4. Look for IT-like tasks at your current job: automating reports, rolling out a CRM, testing internal systems.
5. Start applying once you cover most of the requirements, not all of them.

## Pitfalls

- **Trying to learn everything at once.** Python, design and DevOps in parallel is a fast route to burnout.
- **Quitting without a cushion.** Financial pressure pushes you to accept the first offer or drop your studies.
- **Throwing away past experience.** A "from scratch" résumé hides your main advantage.
- **Expecting quick results.** The switch takes noticeable time, and that is normal.
- **Being embarrassed to be a junior.** Questions and mistakes are part of the process, and experienced colleagues know it.
- **Isolation.** Without a community and feedback it is easy to get stuck. Look for meetups, chats and mentors.

## FAQ

### Is 35 or 40 too late to start?

No. Age matters less than your ability to learn and show results. Mature candidates often stand out through responsibility and communication.

### Should I quit my job right away?

Usually not. It is safer to study in parallel and leave once you have an offer or a sufficient financial cushion.

### Which role should I choose if I am unsure?

Start with the one closest to your current experience. Changing specialization inside IT later is easier than entering the industry from zero.
