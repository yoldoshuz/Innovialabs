## The short answer

A technical interview tests not your memory but **how you solve problems and work with a team**. Preparation comes down to three steps: **learn the format**, **close the gaps for each stage** and **practice talking out loud**. The first step takes one question to the recruiter: "What are the stages, and what does each one assess?" It is a normal question, and the answer can save you weeks of unfocused preparation.

## Typical stages and what they test

| Stage | What it tests |
|---|---|
| Recruiter screen | Motivation, expectations, fit for the role |
| Technical conversation | Language, frameworks, fundamentals (networking, databases, OS) |
| Live coding / algorithms | How you break down a problem, write and check code |
| Take-home assignment | Code quality without time pressure, how you present results |
| System design | Designing a system and reasoning about trade-offs |
| Behavioral interview | Teamwork, ownership, handling conflict |
| Final meeting | Fit with the team and the manager's expectations |

Not every company has every stage. Small teams often stop at a conversation and a take-home task; large ones add algorithms and system design.

## Preparation plan by level

### Junior

- **Language fundamentals**: types, collections, functions, errors, async code, whatever you use every day.
- **Basic algorithms and data structures**: arrays, hash maps, stacks, queues, sorting, complexity analysis.
- **Tools**: Git, HTTP, SQL basics.
- **Your own projects**: be ready to explain every decision in the code you show.

### Middle

- Everything from junior, but deeper: **how things work inside** your framework, database and caching.
- **Code design**: modularity, testing, error handling, refactoring.
- **Simple system design**: APIs, database schema, queues, caching.
- **Stories from experience**: tasks you drove yourself and what you learned.

### Senior

- **System design** as the core stage: scaling, reliability, monitoring, trade-offs between options.
- **Architecture decisions** and their long-term cost.
- **Leadership**: mentoring, code review, improving processes, working with the business side.
- **Depth in your area**: the interviewer will dig until they find the edge of your knowledge.

## How to answer when you do not know

An unfamiliar question is a normal part of the process. Interviewers often go to the edge of your knowledge on purpose. What matters is how you behave there.

1. **Do not make things up.** A confident wrong answer is worse than an honest "I don't know".
2. **Share what you know nearby.** "I haven't used this tool, but I've used a similar one, and there it works like this."
3. **Reason from fundamentals.** "I would guess it works this way, because..." shows how you think.
4. **Say how you would find out.** Docs, a quick experiment, asking a colleague: that is also an answer.
5. **Clarify the question** if it is vague. Often "I don't know" really means "I'm not sure what you are asking".

## How to practice

- **Mock interviews** with a friend or colleague are the best way to get used to thinking out loud.
- **Solve problems with a timer** and narrate your reasoning.
- **Write down questions** after every real interview and close those gaps.
- **Review your own resume**: you can be asked about any line in detail.
- **Prepare your own questions** for the company: processes, the team, goals for the first months.

## Common mistakes

- Preparing for "everything" without learning the format.
- Going silent while solving a problem.
- Memorizing answers without understanding; one follow-up question exposes it.
- Listing technologies on your resume that you cannot talk about.

## FAQ

### How long should I prepare?

It depends on your level, the format and how fresh your knowledge is. Think in terms of gaps rather than time: once you can comfortably pass a mock interview for each stage, you are ready.

### Should I practice algorithms if the company does not use them?

A basic level always helps: it teaches you to reason about complexity and write careful code. But if there is no algorithm stage, spend more time on your stack and on design.

### What should I do after a failed interview?

Right away, write down the questions and moments that felt hard, and ask the recruiter for feedback. A failed interview is the most precise list of what to improve next.
