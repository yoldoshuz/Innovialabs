## The short answer

A clean group rests on three layers of protection:

1. **Telegram settings**: member permissions, slow mode, join requests, limited admin rights.
2. **Bots**: captcha for newcomers, link and keyword filters, automatic mutes and bans.
3. **People and rules**: a short set of rules, clear consequences and moderators who act fast.

No layer works on its own. Bots stop mass spam; people handle the borderline cases.

## Member permissions

The group's **Permissions** section sets default rights for every member. A sensible baseline for a business community:

- **Allow**: text messages, photos, reactions.
- **Restrict**: links, forwards, stickers and GIFs if it's a working group.
- **Disallow**: adding members, changing the group info and photo, pinning messages.

Give admins **only the rights they need**. A moderator needs to delete messages and ban users; keep the right to appoint new admins with one or two owners.

## Slow mode

**Slow mode** limits how often one member can send messages, from a few seconds up to an hour.

- Turn it on in a **large group** where the discussion becomes a flood.
- Use it temporarily during a **live event, launch or incident**, when everyone writes at once.
- Admins are not affected.

## Join requests

With **Approve new members** turned on, people can't enter right away; each one sends a request. This is the most reliable filter against spam accounts.

- Approve requests **manually** if the group is small or closed, for example customers only.
- Or hand them to a **bot**: it receives the request, asks the person a question in private messages and approves it after a correct answer.
- Invite links have their own join request setting, which helps when different links bring different audiences.

## Captcha and anti-spam bots

A **captcha bot** checks that a newcomer is human: press a button or solve a simple task. Fail within the time limit, and the bot removes them.

Anti-spam bots add filters for:

- **links and mentions** from new members during their first days;
- **forwards** from channels and messages with suspicious buttons;
- **keywords** and typical spam phrases;
- **flooding**: many messages in a short time;
- **shared spammer databases** that some bots maintain.

You can use a ready-made moderation bot or build your own when you need special rules, such as checking members against your customer database. In large groups, also check Telegram's built-in **aggressive anti-spam** option in the admin settings.

## Community rules

Rules are a short **pinned post**, not a legal document.

- **What the group is for**: product questions, sharing experience, announcements.
- **What's not allowed**: unapproved ads, insults, off-topic posts, sharing other people's personal data.
- **Consequences**: warning → temporary mute → ban. The same for everyone.
- **Where to go with personal issues**: orders, payments, complaints go to the support bot or private messages, not the group chat.

Don't ask customers to post **order numbers, phone numbers or addresses** in the group. Move those conversations to a private channel.

## How moderators should work

- Split **shifts** so spam doesn't sit there overnight and on weekends.
- When banning, use **delete all messages from this user**; spammers often post several before being caught.
- Answer members' questions. A group where moderators only delete and never help dies off.
- For large communities, turn on **topics** and separate support, discussion and news.

## FAQ

### Will a captcha scare off real members?

A simple one-click captcha barely bothers people and stops spam bots. Complex puzzles and long checks are what annoy users, so avoid them.

### What if spam still gets through?

Look at the pattern: new accounts, links, forwards, specific words. Add the matching filter or turn on join requests.

### Do I need my own bot if ready-made ones exist?

Ready-made bots are enough for most groups. Build your own when rules depend on your data, for example letting in active customers only.
