## How Trello works

Trello is a visual task manager built on three objects:

- **Board** — one project, process or team. For example, "Website redesign" or "Sales pipeline".
- **List** — a column on the board that represents a stage: "To Do", "In Progress", "Done".
- **Card** — one task. It moves from list to list as work progresses.

That is the whole model. Everything else — labels, checklists, automation — is added on top of cards.

## The Kanban idea behind it

Trello is a digital version of a **Kanban board**: sticky notes on a wall split into columns. The method rests on a few simple principles:

- **Make work visible.** Everyone sees what is planned, what is in progress and what is finished without asking.
- **Limit work in progress.** If the "In Progress" list keeps growing, the team starts many things and finishes few. A short list there is a good sign.
- **Pull, do not push.** A person takes the next card when they have capacity, instead of tasks being thrown at them.
- **Improve the flow.** When cards pile up in one list, that stage is your bottleneck.

Trello does not enforce these rules — it only gives you the board. The discipline comes from the team.

## Setting up your first board

1. **Create a board** and name it after the project, not after a person.
2. **Start with three lists**: To Do, In Progress, Done. Add more only when a stage really exists, for example "Review" or "Waiting for client".
3. **Add cards** with short, action-based titles: "Write the pricing page text", not "Pricing".
4. **Invite members** and assign each card to the person responsible. One card — one owner.
5. **Agree on the rules**: what "Done" means, who moves cards, how often the board is reviewed.

A good test: a new team member should understand the state of the project from the board in under a minute.

## Labels, checklists and due dates

These three features cover most everyday needs:

| Feature | What it is for | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| **Labels** | Color tags for type, priority or area: "bug", "urgent", "design" | Keep a short list and name every color, otherwise nobody remembers what green means |
| **Checklists** | Subtasks inside a card | Use them for steps of one task; if a step has its own owner, make it a separate card |
| **Due dates** | Deadline, optionally with a start date and reminder | Set dates only where a real deadline exists; red overdue cards everywhere stop meaning anything |

Cards also hold **descriptions**, **attachments** and **comments**. Keep discussion in card comments instead of chats — then the history of a task lives in one place.

## Power-Ups: when to add them

**Power-Ups** are add-ons that extend a board: a calendar, custom fields, integrations with Google Drive, Slack, GitHub and other services.

How to use them wisely:

- Add a Power-Up to solve a specific problem you already have, not "just in case".
- **Custom Fields** are useful when every card needs the same data — budget, client, estimate.
- Integrations save time when your files or messages already live in another service.
- Review installed Power-Ups from time to time and remove unused ones.

## What the free plan limits

The free plan is enough for personal use and small teams, but it has limits. They usually concern:

- the number of boards in a Workspace and the number of collaborators;
- the maximum size of an attached file;
- the number of automation runs per month;
- additional views such as Timeline, Table and Dashboard, which belong to paid plans;
- admin and security controls for larger organizations.

The exact numbers change from time to time, so check the official pricing page before you plan a team rollout.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Too many lists.** Ten columns for a three-person team turn the board into bureaucracy.
- **Cards without owners.** If everyone is responsible, nobody is.
- **One giant board for everything.** Split by project or team when the board becomes hard to scan.
- **Never archiving.** Archive finished cards regularly so "Done" does not become a graveyard.
- **Board as a to-do dump.** Cards should be concrete tasks, not ideas or notes.

## FAQ

### Is Trello suitable for software development?

For small teams and simple processes, yes. When you need sprints, story points, detailed reports and complex workflows, tools like Jira usually fit better.

### What is the difference between a label and a list?

A list shows the **stage** of work, so a card is in only one list at a time. A label describes a **property** of the card, and one card can have several labels.

### Can I use Trello alone?

Yes. Many people run personal boards for study, job search or household plans. The same three lists work just as well for one person.
