## The short answer

A **backlink** is a link from someone else's website to yours. To a search engine it works like a recommendation: if other sites link to a page, it is probably useful.

Links were one of the first ranking signals and they still matter. But search engines have long learned to tell genuine recommendations from artificial ones, so "more links = higher rankings" no longer works.

## How links pass authority

Every page accumulates a certain amount of trust. When it links to another page, part of that trust flows on. A few practical consequences:

- a link from an authoritative page is worth more than one from an obscure page;
- if a page has hundreds of outgoing links, each one receives less;
- links work at the **page** level, not only the domain level: a link to a specific article strengthens that article first;
- **internal links** distribute the accumulated weight across your own site.

## What makes a link valuable

| Criterion | Strong link | Weak link |
|---|---|---|
| **Relevance** | a site and page from your topic | a random site unrelated to your niche |
| **Source trust** | a known publication, industry resource, partner | a site built to sell links |
| **Placement** | inside article text, in context | footer, sidebar, a list of a hundred links |
| **Anchor text** | natural: brand name, what the page is about | the same commercial phrase everywhere |
| **Traffic** | real people click it | nobody ever sees it |

A good mental test: **would you want this link if search engines did not exist?** If a link brings readers and helps the linking site's audience, it is most likely valuable for SEO too.

## The nofollow, sponsored and ugc attributes

A site owner can mark a link with a special attribute:

```html
<a href="https://example.com" rel="nofollow">...</a>
<a href="https://example.com" rel="sponsored">...</a>
<a href="https://example.com" rel="ugc">...</a>
```

- `sponsored` — for paid and advertising links;
- `ugc` — for links in comments and forums;
- `nofollow` — a general "do not pass trust" signal.

Google treats these attributes as hints. Such links are still useful: they bring traffic and make the brand more visible.

## Why quantity does not work

- **Search engines recognise schemes.** Private networks, link marketplaces, bulk directory submissions — algorithms are trained to find and discount them.
- **Penalties are possible.** Google and Yandex explicitly treat buying links to manipulate rankings as a violation. Consequences range from links being ignored to the site being demoted.
- **One good link can outweigh many weak ones.** A mention in an industry publication brings both weight and an audience; hundreds of junk links bring neither.

## How to earn links honestly

1. **Create something worth linking to.** Research on your own data, useful guides, calculators, templates.
2. **Work with partners.** Suppliers, clients and integrators are often happy to link to a page about joint work.
3. **Give expert comments to media** and speak at industry events.
4. **Find unlinked mentions.** If someone wrote about you without a link, politely ask them to add one.
5. **List yourself in quality industry directories** and on maps — without bulk submissions.

## FAQ

### How many links do I need to reach the top?

There is no universal number. It depends on competition for the query and the quality of links that top-ranking sites have. Compare the quality and relevance of competitors' linking sites, not the count.

### Can bad links hurt my site?

Usually search engines simply ignore junk links. The risk appears when a site buys links in bulk itself: that can lead to manual actions and lost rankings.

### Where can I see links to my site?

A basic list is available in Google Search Console (the Links report) and in Yandex Webmaster. Third-party SEO tools show more data, but their databases are incomplete and differ from one another.
