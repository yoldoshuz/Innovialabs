## Design thinking in one paragraph

**Design thinking** is a way of solving problems by starting from the people who have them. Instead of jumping to a solution, a team first learns how users actually behave, formulates the real problem, generates several ideas, builds quick prototypes and tests them. The stages are commonly described as **empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test**, and they repeat in cycles rather than run once in a straight line.

## The five stages

| Stage | Goal | Typical activities | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empathize** | Understand users and their context | Interviews, observation, support tickets, analytics | Notes, quotes, observed behaviour |
| **Define** | Formulate the right problem | Grouping findings, problem statement | One clear problem to solve |
| **Ideate** | Generate many possible solutions | Brainstorming, sketching, "how might we" questions | A shortlist of ideas |
| **Prototype** | Make ideas tangible cheaply | Paper sketches, clickable mockups in Figma | Something users can try |
| **Test** | Learn what works | Usability sessions with real users | Evidence to iterate or move forward |

## A realistic example: booking at a clinic

Imagine a private clinic whose online booking form gets visits but few completed bookings. The first instinct is "redesign the form".

**Empathize.** The team talks to several patients and to the reception staff, and watches people try to book. They notice that many users do not know which doctor they need; they know only their symptom. Others drop off when asked to create an account.

**Define.** The problem is reframed from "the form is ugly" to: *patients who know their symptom but not the specialist cannot choose a doctor and leave before booking*. A good problem statement names the user, the need and the obstacle, without dictating the solution.

**Ideate.** The team turns this into the question "How might we help patients reach the right doctor without medical knowledge?" Ideas include: choosing by symptom, a short guided questionnaire, booking a general consultation first, a callback request, guest booking without registration.

**Prototype.** Two ideas are mocked up as clickable screens: a symptom-based picker and a guest booking flow. No code, only enough fidelity to test.

**Test.** A handful of people from the target audience try both prototypes with a realistic task: "Book a visit for back pain this week." The team watches where they hesitate. Perhaps the symptom picker works but its wording confuses people, and guest booking removes a major barrier. The next iteration refines both.

The final solution may look nothing like the "prettier form" the team started with — that is the point.

## Common ways teams misapply it

- **Workshop theatre.** A day of sticky notes with no real users involved. Without research, empathize becomes guessing.
- **Skipping define.** Teams jump from interviews straight to ideas and solve a symptom instead of the problem.
- **Falling in love with the first idea.** Ideation exists to compare options; one idea is not a choice.
- **Over-polished prototypes.** Spending weeks on a high-fidelity mockup makes it emotionally hard to throw away and slows learning.
- **Testing with colleagues.** Teammates know the product too well. Test with people who match your audience.
- **Treating it as linear.** Tests often send you back to define or ideate. That is a success, not a failure.
- **Stopping at insights.** Design thinking must end in decisions and changes in the product, not just a presentation.

## When it is worth using

Design thinking pays off when the problem is unclear, users' behaviour is poorly understood, or the cost of building the wrong thing is high: new products, new features, redesigns of key flows. For a small, well-understood change, a lighter process is usually enough.

A practical minimum for most teams: talk to real users, write one problem statement, sketch several options, test the cheapest prototype that answers your question.

## FAQ

### Is design thinking only for designers?

No. It works best with a mixed team: product managers, developers, designers and people who talk to customers, such as support or sales. Different perspectives produce better problem definitions and ideas.

### How long does one design thinking cycle take?

It depends on the scope of the problem and access to users. A focused question can go through all five stages quickly, while a new product may need several cycles. The pace is set by how fast you can talk to users and test.

### What is the difference between design thinking and UX design?

Design thinking is a general problem-solving mindset and process. UX design is a discipline that designs specific products and interfaces and often uses design thinking stages as part of its work.
