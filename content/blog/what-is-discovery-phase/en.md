## The short answer

A **discovery phase** is the stage before development where the team works with you to figure out exactly what to build, for whom and how. The result is a set of documents and prototypes that allow an accurate estimate and a start without costly rework.

It saves money for a simple reason: changing a requirement in a document takes minutes, while rewriting a finished feature takes days or weeks.

## Goals of discovery

- **Understand the business problem.** What the product solves and how you will know it succeeded.
- **Get to know the users.** Who they are, their scenarios and constraints.
- **Define the scope.** What goes into the first version and what can wait.
- **Find technical risks.** Integrations, load, security requirements, third-party services.
- **Produce a realistic estimate** of time and cost.

## What you get at the end

The exact set depends on the project, but usually includes:

- a description of goals and success metrics;
- user personas and a user flow map;
- a **backlog** — a prioritized feature list, often with an MVP scope marked;
- wireframes or a clickable prototype of key screens;
- an architecture and tech stack description;
- a list of integrations and risks;
- a time and budget estimate broken down by phase.

Important: these materials are yours. You can take them to any vendor.

## How the phase runs

1. **Interviews** with you and, where possible, future users.
2. **Analysis** of competitors, existing processes and systems.
3. **Workshops** to align on scenarios and priorities.
4. **Prototyping** of key screens.
5. **Technical design** and estimation.
6. **Presentation** of results and a plan for next steps.

## What drives duration and cost

Quoting numbers without context is meaningless, so look at the factors:

- **product size and complexity** — number of roles, platforms and scenarios;
- **number of integrations** — payment systems, CRM, ERP, external APIs;
- **depth of work** — wireframes only or a full design concept;
- **availability of information** — how quickly you respond and grant access;
- **team composition** — analyst, designer, architect, project manager.

Discovery usually takes a small share of the total project budget and is billed separately, often at a fixed price.

## How discovery reduces estimate errors

Without research, an estimate is built on assumptions. The vendor either adds a large buffer for the unknown or underestimates the scope, and the budget grows mid-project.

After discovery, uncertainty is much lower: the feature set is clear, integrations are known and technical risks are visible. The estimate range becomes narrower, and there are fewer changes during development.

## When discovery matters most

- you are building a new product or an MVP;
- requirements are still described in general terms;
- the project has many integrations or complex business logic;
- you are choosing between vendors and want to compare them on the same basis.

For small tasks with a ready spec and design, a separate phase may not be needed.

## FAQ

### Can I skip discovery?

Yes, if you already have a detailed spec, design and a clear scope. Otherwise the missing decisions will still have to be made, just later during development and at a higher cost.

### What if I decide not to build the product after discovery?

That is a valid outcome too: you spent a small part of the budget and avoided large costs on a product you do not need.

### Can I continue development with a different team?

Yes. The documents, prototypes and estimate are yours and can be handed to any vendor.
