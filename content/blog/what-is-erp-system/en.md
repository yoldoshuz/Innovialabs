## The short answer

**ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning)** is a system for managing a company's resources: money, goods, materials, equipment and people. Its core idea is **one shared database** for every department.

When the warehouse ships an order, accounting immediately sees the revenue and cost, purchasing sees the lower stock level, and management sees up-to-date profit. Nobody emails files around or reconciles numbers by hand.

Examples of ERP systems include SAP, Oracle NetSuite, Microsoft Dynamics, Odoo and, in CIS countries, products built on the 1C platform (such as 1C:ERP).

## Typical ERP modules

### Finance and accounting

General ledger, payables and receivables, bank and cash, taxes, financial reporting. Almost every other module eventually posts entries here.

### Procurement

Purchase requests, supplier selection, purchase orders, goods receipt, price and lead-time control. The module knows what to reorder and when, based on stock and sales data.

### Warehouse and inventory

Stock by warehouse and bin, receipts, transfers, stock counts, batches and expiry dates, reserving stock for orders.

### Sales and orders

Customer orders, price lists, shipments, invoices. Some ERPs include CRM features, but usually simpler than a dedicated CRM.

### Manufacturing

Bills of materials (what each product is made of), routings, capacity planning, recording output and material consumption.

### HR and payroll

Employees, positions, timesheets, payroll, leave. Staff costs flow into finance and product cost.

Companies rarely roll out every module at once. They usually start with finance, inventory and procurement and add the rest later.

## How modules share one data model

Follow a single order through the system:

1. A salesperson creates a **sales order**, and the system reserves stock.
2. If stock is short, a **purchase requirement** or a **production order** appears.
3. Production consumes **materials from the warehouse** and outputs finished goods.
4. The warehouse **ships** the order, and stock goes down.
5. Finance automatically receives the **revenue, cost of goods and receivable**.
6. When the customer pays, the receivable closes and management sees current cash flow.

Each document — order, receipt, shipment — is created once and used by everyone. Master data for products, customers, suppliers and employees is shared too. That is what separates an ERP from a collection of separate programs.

## Signs you have outgrown spreadsheets

- Stock levels in Excel do not match reality, and every stock count brings surprises.
- The same data is entered several times: in the sales sheet, the warehouse log and the accounting system.
- Finding last month's profit takes accounting weeks of merging files from different teams.
- Purchasing is done by gut feeling because nobody knows what is in transit or reserved.
- Product cost is only an estimate.
- You now have several warehouses, legal entities or branches, and combining their data by hand is hard.
- Everything depends on one person who "knows which file is where".

If several of these sound familiar, it is time to look at an ERP, or at least at connecting your accounting system with inventory.

## How to approach the choice

- **Start with processes**, not vendors: describe how money and goods move today.
- **Check local requirements**: tax reporting, electronic invoices and accounting rules in your country.
- **Check integrations**: website, marketplaces, CRM, bank, payment providers.
- **Roll out in phases**: critical modules first, the rest later.
- **Plan time for training**: an ERP only works if everyone uses it.

Cost and timeline depend on the number of modules, the amount of customization, the number of integrations, the quality of your existing data and the size of the company, so there is no single price.

## FAQ

### Does a small company need an ERP?

Not always. A small business often does fine with accounting software and simple inventory tracking. ERP becomes necessary when processes and headcount grow and manual reconciliation starts eating time.

### Can I implement only some modules?

Yes, most ERP systems are modular. Companies usually start with finance, inventory and procurement and add manufacturing or HR later.

### How is an ERP different from accounting software?

Accounting software keeps the books for accountants and tax authorities. An ERP covers the whole operation — purchasing, inventory, production, planning — and accounting becomes one part of it.
