## The short answer

**Information architecture (IA)** is the structure of a product: what content it contains, how that content is grouped, what the groups are called and which paths people take between them.

If a website were a building, visual design would be the finishes and furniture, while IA would be the floor plan: entrances, hallways, signs and which rooms sit next to each other. Beautiful finishes won't save a building where you can't find the exit.

Bad IA is easy to spot: someone opens the menu, scans the items and has no idea where to click. Good IA usually goes unnoticed, because things are simply where people expect them.

## What IA is made of

IA is usually described as several systems working together.

### Organization system

How content is split into groups. The main approaches:

- **By topic**: "Shipping," "Payment," "Returns."
- **By audience**: "For business," "For individuals."
- **By task**: "Open an account," "Send money."
- **Alphabetical, chronological or geographic**: when people already know exactly what they want, as in a directory, an archive or a list of branches.

The key is to pick **logic that makes sense to users** rather than mirroring the company's org chart. Users don't care which department handles shipping.

### Labeling system

What sections, buttons and links are called. A good label:

- uses the user's language, not internal jargon;
- is unambiguous, so it's clear what's inside;
- is consistent, so the same thing has the same name everywhere.

Having "Solutions," "Products" and "Services" on one site is a classic source of confusion: people can't tell how they differ.

### Navigation system

How people move through the structure:

- **Global**: the main menu, available everywhere.
- **Local**: movement within a section.
- **Contextual**: links inside content, such as "Related products" or "Read next."
- **Supplementary**: breadcrumbs, footer, search.

### Search

In large products search is its own system: what gets indexed, how results are shown, what happens when nothing is found. Search complements a clear structure; it doesn't replace it.

## Sitemaps and user flows

These are the two main artifacts that put IA on paper.

| Artifact | What it shows | When you need it |
|---|---|---|
| **Sitemap** | The hierarchy of all pages and sections as a tree | To see the whole structure and agree on what sections exist |
| **User flow** | A user's path through screens toward a goal, including branches and errors | To design a specific scenario: sign-up, checkout, booking |

A sitemap answers "what lives where." A user flow answers "how does a person reach their goal." You usually need both.

## How to design IA: steps

1. **Inventory the content.** List every page, feature and piece of material the product should contain.
2. **Understand your users.** What tasks do they have, and what words do they use to describe what they look for?
3. **Run a card sort.** Participants group topic cards and name the groups, revealing their mental model.
4. **Draw a sitemap** based on what you learned.
5. **Validate with tree testing.** Participants see only a text tree of sections and are asked where they'd find something. This tests the structure separately from visual design.
6. **Map the key user flows**, and only then move on to mockups.

## Common mistakes

- The structure mirrors the company's internal setup instead of user tasks.
- Nesting is too deep, so reaching anything takes many levels.
- The menu is too wide, with dozens of items on one level that are hard to choose between.
- Vague labels like "Miscellaneous," "Resources" or "Useful stuff."
- The same content lives in several places under different names.
- IA is invented while drawing mockups rather than before.

## FAQ

### How is information architecture different from UX design?

IA is part of UX design. UX covers the entire experience of using a product, while IA is specifically about structure, labels and navigation. It's the foundation the mockups are built on.

### Does a small website need IA?

Yes, in a simple form. Even for a landing page or a few-page site, it's worth deciding which sections exist, what they're called and in what order people will see them. It takes little time and saves rework.

### What's the difference between card sorting and tree testing?

Card sorting helps you **create** a structure by showing how people naturally group content. Tree testing helps you **validate** an existing structure by checking whether people can find things in the proposed tree of sections.
