## What an IT audit is

An **IT audit** is a review of how a company's IT systems are built and run: which software is used, what state the code and servers are in, how much it all costs, and what the risks are. The result is not just a list of problems but a **clear action plan** with priorities.

An audit can be full or focused on one area: for example, only security or only the code quality of a specific product.

## What gets checked

| Area | What is reviewed |
|---|---|
| **Systems and processes** | Which services are used, how they connect, duplication and manual data transfer |
| **Code** | Architecture, readability, tests, outdated dependencies, documentation |
| **Infrastructure** | Servers, hosting, backups, monitoring, deployment process |
| **Security** | Access rights, passwords, updates, storage of personal data |
| **Costs** | Subscriptions, licences, hosting, unused resources |
| **Risks** | Dependence on one contractor or employee, missing access and documentation |

## When a company needs an IT audit

Typical triggers:

- **Changing contractors.** You need to know what is being handed over and in what condition.
- **The product has become slow or unstable**, and the cause is unclear.
- **IT costs are growing**, but it is not clear what exactly you are paying for.
- **Scaling or a major upgrade is planned**, and you need to know whether the current foundation will hold.
- **There was an incident**: a breach, data loss, a long outage.
- **An acquisition or investment** requires checking the technical side before the deal.
- **No access or documentation**: the company does not control its own systems.

## How an audit works

1. **Set goals.** What worries you and which decisions need to be made afterwards.
2. **Collect access and materials**: repositories, servers, services, contracts, documentation.
3. **Interviews** with the people who use and maintain the systems.
4. **Technical review**: analysis of code, configurations, logs and security settings.
5. **Cost analysis** and resource usage.
6. **Report and discussion** of the findings.

## What the final report should contain

A good report is understandable not only to technical staff. It usually includes:

- an **executive summary** for management: the overall state and key risks;
- a **list of issues** with descriptions, consequences and severity;
- **recommendations**: what to do, with what resources and in what order;
- **priorities**: what is urgent, what is important, what can wait;
- a **systems map**: what exists, how it connects, who has access;
- appendices with technical details for the team.

A bad sign is a report full of generic phrases like "the code needs refactoring" without specific places and consequences.

## Common mistakes

- Running an audit without a goal, which produces a long document nobody uses.
- Asking the same team that built the system to audit it, with no independent view.
- Expecting the audit to fix everything: it is a diagnosis, and fixes are a separate stage.
- Not giving the auditor access, which leaves the conclusions incomplete.

## FAQ

### How long does an IT audit take?

It depends on scale: the number of systems, the amount of code, and how quickly access is provided. A focused audit of one area is faster than a full one.

### Can an audit be done without access to the code?

Partly: you can assess how the product behaves, the infrastructure from the outside, costs and processes. But conclusions about code quality and risks will be superficial without repository access.

### How often should an IT audit be done?

There is no fixed rule. It makes sense around significant events: a change of contractor, growing load, incidents, or before major investment in the product.
