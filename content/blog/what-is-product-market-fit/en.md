## A plain definition

**Product-market fit (PMF)** means your product matches a market: you found a group of people with a real problem, and your product solves it well enough that they use it regularly, recommend it and are willing to pay.

The key is a **specific segment**. PMF is never "for everyone". It is always "for these customers in this situation".

## Qualitative signals

You see them in customer conversations and in how the team works:

- Users **would be upset if the product disappeared** and can explain why.
- Customers arrive through referrals, not only ads.
- Feature requests become specific and similar to each other.
- Sales get easier: less explaining why the product is needed.
- Support is busy not with "I can't figure out how to use it" but with requests for more.

A useful tool is the **Sean Ellis survey**: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" with options "very disappointed", "somewhat disappointed" and "not disappointed". What matters is the share of "very disappointed" answers and who exactly those people are.

## Quantitative signals

| Metric | What it shows | Good sign |
|---|---|---|
| Retention | Whether users come back | Cohort curve flattens instead of dropping to zero |
| Organic growth | Whether the product grows without ads | A noticeable share of new customers from referrals and search |
| Repeat purchases | Whether people come back to pay | A stable share of repeat orders |
| Churn | How many customers leave | Decreases cohort over cohort |
| Acquisition cost | What a new customer costs | Pays back through revenue per customer |

The most reliable signal is the **retention curve**. If each new cohort keeps a portion of users for the long run, someone truly needs the product.

## Why scaling before PMF is dangerous

Scaling means amplifying: more ads, more salespeople, more features, more servers. If the market does not need the product yet, you amplify the **problem**, not the result.

- Ads bring people who leave after the first visit.
- The team grows while the speed of finding the right product drops.
- New features add complexity without solving the core job.
- Money runs out before you understand what is wrong.

Before PMF, the main job is to **learn fast and cheaply**: short cycles, an MVP, customer conversations, targeted changes.

## How to move toward PMF

1. **Narrow the segment.** Pick one customer group with a sharp problem.
2. **State the value.** One sentence: which problem you solve and for whom.
3. **Launch an MVP.** A minimal version that solves the core job.
4. **Measure retention**, not just sign-ups.
5. **Talk to those who stayed and those who left.** Both give answers.
6. **Change one thing at a time.** Then you know what worked.

## Common mistakes

- Mistaking a sign-up spike after launch for PMF.
- Watching **vanity metrics**: page views, downloads, followers.
- Surveying friends instead of real customers.
- Assuming PMF is reached once and for all. Markets change, and fit can be lost.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to find product-market fit?

There is no universal timeline. It depends on the market, product complexity and how quickly the team tests hypotheses. Short cycles and honest metrics speed things up considerably.

### Can you reach PMF without paying customers?

For free products, retention and organic growth are the main signals. But if the business model relies on payment, test willingness to pay as early as possible.

### What if there are no PMF signals for a long time?

Revisit the segment or the problem rather than just adding features. Sometimes the product fits a different audience than planned, which shows in who stays.
