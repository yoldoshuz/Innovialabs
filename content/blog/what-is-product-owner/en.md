## The short answer

A **Product Owner** is the person accountable for *what* the team builds and *in which order*. They don't write code and don't manage developers directly. Their area is the value of the product for the business and its users.

The role comes from Scrum, but today the title is used for anyone on a project who:

- defines the product goals;
- maintains and orders the **backlog**, the list of tasks and ideas;
- accepts or rejects finished work;
- says "yes" or "no" to new requirements.

The key word is **one person**. Not a committee, not a department, but a specific individual with the authority to decide.

## What they do in practice

**Set the goal.** Why the product exists, who it is for and how you know it works. Without this, the team ships features instead of outcomes.

**Prioritize.** There are always more ideas than time and money. The Product Owner decides what goes into the next release and what waits.

**Write and refine requirements.** Not necessarily a long specification. Often these are short user stories with acceptance criteria: what must work for the task to count as done.

**Answer the team's questions.** Developers constantly hit forks: "what if the user...". A quick answer saves days.

**Accept the work.** Watch demos, check results against the criteria and give feedback.

**Connect with the business.** Collect input from management, sales and support, and turn it into clear tasks.

## Their decision rights

The role only works when the person has real authority:

| Decision | Who makes it |
|---|---|
| Backlog order | Product Owner |
| What goes into a release | Product Owner |
| Whether a task is accepted | Product Owner |
| How to implement it technically | Development team |
| How long a task will take | Development team |
| Company budget and strategy | Leadership |

If every Product Owner decision is later overruled by a director, the role becomes a message relay and all the problems come back.

## Why the client side needs one

A contractor can provide a project manager or an analyst, but **the product owner should sit on the business side**. Only the client knows its customers, processes and constraints. A contractor can help shape a solution, but cannot decide for you what matters most to your company.

## What happens without a Product Owner

Typical symptoms:

- **Decisions hang for weeks.** The team waits while a question bounces between several managers.
- **Conflicting requirements.** Sales asks for one thing, accounting for another, and both believe it was agreed.
- **Endless rework.** Without clear acceptance criteria the result is "not right", yet nobody can say what right looks like.
- **Scope bloat.** Everything feels important, so the first version gets overloaded and the launch slips.
- **Budget overrun.** Team time goes into waiting and rework, and you still pay for it.

## How to pick a Product Owner in your company

Look for someone who:

1. **Knows the business process** the product supports.
2. **Can decide** without constant escalation.
3. **Has time for it**. This is not a "couple of minutes a week" job.
4. **Can say "no"** and explain why.
5. **Is reachable** by the team at least daily for short questions.

A technical background helps but is not required. Understanding users and expressing ideas clearly matter more.

## Common mistakes

- Giving the role "on the side" to a busy executive with no time for it.
- Splitting the role between two or three people.
- Handing it entirely to the contractor.
- Confusing a Product Owner with a project manager: the latter tracks deadlines and process, the former owns value and priorities.

## FAQ

### Can the Product Owner be on the contractor's side?

A contractor can help with analysis and writing requirements, but final priority decisions are best left to someone on the client side. Otherwise the product is built on someone else's understanding of your business.

### Does a small project need a Product Owner?

Yes, though it may simply be the owner or a department head. The title matters less than having one person who makes decisions quickly.

### How much time does the role take?

It depends on project size and team pace. At minimum: regular participation in planning and demos plus prompt answers to questions. During active development it is a noticeable share of the working week.
