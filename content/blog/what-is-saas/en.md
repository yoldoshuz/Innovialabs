## What SaaS means in plain words

**SaaS (Software as a Service)** is a model where you do not buy and install a program — you get access to it over the internet and pay for usage. Familiar examples: a cloud CRM, online accounting, an email marketing service, a task tracker.

The key idea: **the software runs on the provider's servers**. You open a browser or an app, sign in and use it. Installation, updates, backups and infrastructure security are the provider's job.

## How the model works

- **Subscription.** You pay monthly or yearly. The price usually depends on the number of users, data volume or feature set (plans).
- **One system, many customers.** Usually all customers use the same application, but each customer's data is isolated. This is called **multi-tenancy**.
- **Continuous updates.** New features reach everyone at once, with no reinstallation.
- **Access from anywhere.** All you need is the internet and a login.

### How SaaS differs from other models

| Model | What you get | Who runs the servers |
|---|---|---|
| Boxed software (on-premise) | A license and a local install | You |
| IaaS | Virtual servers | You, for everything above the hardware |
| PaaS | A platform to run your own code | Provider runs the platform, you own the code |
| SaaS | A ready application | The provider, for everything |

## Pros and cons for buyers

**Pros:**

- Fast start — sign up and get to work.
- No upfront spending on servers and licenses.
- Updates and support are included.
- Easy to scale: add a user, add a seat to your plan.

**Cons:**

- **Vendor dependency.** If the service raises prices, changes features or shuts down, your process takes the hit.
- **Your data lives elsewhere.** You need to know where the servers are and how to export your data.
- **Limited customization.** The product is built for a mass market; it will not be rewritten around your unique process.
- **Subscriptions add up.** Over time, a dozen services may cost more than one system of your own.

### What to check before buying

1. Can you **export your data** in an open format?
2. Is there an **API** to integrate with your other systems?
3. Where is the data stored, and does that meet local personal data requirements?
4. What availability and support terms are in the contract (SLA)?
5. What happens to your data after you cancel?

## Pros and cons for SaaS founders

**Why the model is attractive:**

- **Recurring revenue** — income is more predictable than with one-off sales.
- One codebase serves every customer, which simplifies maintenance.
- You can ship improvements quickly and see how people use them.

**What you will face:**

- **Churn.** A subscription is easy to cancel, so the product must deliver value every month.
- **Slow payback.** Development and customer acquisition costs come upfront, revenue arrives in installments.
- **Infrastructure responsibility.** An outage hits all customers at once. You need monitoring, backups and on-call duty.
- **Security and data isolation** — a mistake in access control can expose one customer's data to another.

### What a SaaS product is made of

- A web app and, if needed, a mobile app.
- Accounts, roles and permissions.
- **Billing**: plans, subscriptions, trials, payment provider integration.
- An admin panel for your team.
- Infrastructure: servers or cloud, monitoring, backups.

## Common mistakes when launching SaaS

- Building complex billing and dozens of plans before the first paying customers.
- Not designing customer data isolation from day one.
- Ignoring onboarding: users who do not get the product in the first days leave.
- Not measuring churn and the reasons people cancel.

## FAQ

### How is SaaS different from a regular website?

A website mostly presents information. SaaS is a working tool with accounts, user data and features people pay for on a subscription.

### Can you move from SaaS to your own system?

Yes, if the service lets you export your data. Companies usually switch when their processes outgrow the ready product or subscriptions cost more than maintaining their own system.

### Should a SaaS target every market from the start?

No. It is wiser to start with an MVP for a narrow audience, confirm people are willing to pay, and only then expand features and plans.
