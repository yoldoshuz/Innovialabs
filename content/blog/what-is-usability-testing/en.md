## The short answer

**Usability testing** is a method where you give real users specific tasks in your product and watch how they complete them. For example: "Order delivery for tomorrow" or "Find out how much the team plan costs."

The key word is **observation, not opinion**. People are bad at predicting their own behavior and will happily say "it's all clear" right after clicking the wrong thing five times. A test shows what people actually do.

You can test almost anything: a clickable Figma prototype, a live website, a mobile app, even paper sketches of screens.

## Moderated vs unmoderated

| Format | How it works | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Moderated** | A facilitator is present in the room or on a call, gives tasks and asks follow-up questions | You can ask "why?", dig deeper and adapt the script | Takes facilitator time, harder to scale |
| **Unmoderated** | The participant completes tasks alone through a testing tool while the session is recorded | Fast, easy to reach more people, no facilitator bias | No follow-up questions, tasks must be worded perfectly |

Moderated sessions work best early, when you don't yet know where the problems are. Unmoderated sessions are useful when you want to check a specific flow with more people.

## Remote vs in-person

- **In-person**: the participant and facilitator share a room. You see facial expressions and body language, and it's easy to help with equipment. Good for physical devices and for audiences who struggle with video calls.
- **Remote**: over a video call with screen sharing. Cheaper and faster, lets you reach people in other cities, and the participant uses their own device in a familiar setting.

These two dimensions are independent: a remote test can be moderated (a call with a facilitator) or unmoderated (a recorded session through a tool).

## What problems it uncovers

- **Confusing navigation**: people can't find a section or look for it somewhere other than where you put it.
- **Unclear wording**: a "Continue" button leads somewhere unexpected; jargon only the team understands.
- **Invisible elements**: a key button blends into the background or sits below the fold.
- **Form friction**: unclear password rules, data wiped after an error, awkward phone number input.
- **False expectations**: the user believes something was saved when it wasn't.

Usability testing does not tell you whether people want the product, and it does not replace analytics. It answers a narrower question: can people use it?

## Why five users are often enough

The idea was popularized by **Jakob Nielsen** of Nielsen Norman Group. The logic is simple: the first participant reveals many problems, the second repeats some and adds a few new ones, the third adds even fewer. With each additional person you mostly see repeats.

That's why several **small rounds** beat one big test with 20 people: test with five, fix what you found, test again. Each round surfaces problems that were previously hidden behind bigger ones.

Two important caveats:

- Five people refers to **one homogeneous group**. If your product serves different audiences (buyers and sellers), each needs its own sample.
- The rule applies to **qualitative** problem-finding. Quantitative metrics such as task time or success rate need more participants.

## How to run your first test

1. **Define the goal.** What are you checking: checkout, sign-up, search?
2. **Write 3 to 5 tasks** as realistic scenarios without hints. Not "click Cart," but "you've picked a product, now buy it."
3. **Recruit participants** from your target audience, not colleagues from the next desk.
4. **Ask them to think aloud** so you hear what they expect to see.
5. **Don't help.** If someone is stuck, ask "what did you expect?" instead of pointing to the button.
6. **Record observations, not interpretations**: "looked for payment in the profile menu," not "the menu is bad."
7. **List the problems** and prioritize them by how badly they block the task.

## Common mistakes

- Testing on colleagues and friends who know the context and avoid criticism.
- Asking leading questions like "That was easy, right?"
- Testing only at the very end, when changes are expensive.
- Drawing conclusions from a single participant.
- Running the test and never fixing what it found.

## FAQ

### How is usability testing different from A/B testing?

An A/B test compares two versions on large traffic and shows which performs better on a metric, but not why. A usability test with a small group reveals the reasons: where and why people get stuck.

### Can I test before the product exists?

Yes, and it's the most cost-effective moment. A clickable prototype or even paper sketches let you find problems before any development time is spent on them.

### How often should we test?

It helps to build short rounds into every notable stage: after the prototype, before launch and after major interface changes. Small, regular tests are more useful than rare large ones.
