## The short answer

A **mobile developer** builds applications for smartphones and tablets. They are responsible for what users see and touch and for what happens on the device behind the scenes: screens and navigation, talking to servers through APIs, local data storage, notifications, camera and location access, performance and battery use.

A separate part of the job is **publishing**: preparing builds, passing App Store and Google Play review and shipping updates.

## Main specializations

| Track | Languages and tools | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| iOS (native) | Swift, SwiftUI, UIKit, Xcode | requires a Mac |
| Android (native) | Kotlin, Jetpack Compose, Android Studio | huge variety of devices |
| Flutter | Dart | one codebase for iOS and Android |
| React Native | JavaScript/TypeScript, React | close to web development |

**Native development** gives full access to platform capabilities and usually the best performance. It is chosen for complex apps where smoothness, deep system integration and new OS features matter.

**Cross-platform development** lets one team ship to both platforms at once. Startups, studios and companies focused on speed and budget often choose it.

## How it looks from the hiring side

- **Large product companies** and banks often run separate iOS and Android teams and hire native developers.
- **Studios and startups** more often use Flutter or React Native to ship on both platforms faster.
- **Cross-platform developers** still need native fundamentals: permissions, the app lifecycle, publishing. Without them, unusual problems are hard to solve.
- **React Native** is a natural path if you already know React; **Flutter** is a popular choice for beginners thanks to its cohesive tooling.

Before choosing, open job listings in your city or on remote job boards and see which stacks appear most often where you plan to work. Demand varies noticeably by region and changes over time.

## What a junior needs

**Required basics:**

- solid knowledge of your platform's language;
- building interfaces and navigation between screens;
- working with REST APIs and JSON, handling network errors;
- local data storage;
- asynchronous code: keeping the UI responsive during long operations;
- Git and teamwork.

**Significantly improves your chances:**

- understanding architecture patterns (for example, MVVM) and keeping code organized;
- basic unit tests;
- having published at least one app to a store;
- attention to UI details: loading states, empty screens, errors.

## How to build a portfolio

Build **2–3 finished apps** rather than ten unfinished ones. A good set:

1. An app that uses a public API: list, detail screen, search, error handling.
2. An app with local data: notes, habit tracker, shopping list.
3. Something that solves a real problem of yours; these projects are the easiest to talk about in interviews.

Put the code on GitHub with a clear README, screenshots and a short description of the architecture. If you can publish an app to a store, that is a strong plus.

## Common mistakes

- Learning Swift, Kotlin and Flutter at the same time instead of getting good at one.
- Copying tutorials unchanged and presenting them as your own projects.
- Ignoring error handling and different screen sizes.
- Not understanding how the app communicates with the server.

## FAQ

### Native or cross-platform: what should a beginner choose?

Look at job listings in your region and at your background. If you know JavaScript and React, consider React Native. If you are starting from zero, Flutter or one native platform both work. What matters is reaching a confident level in one of them.

### Do I need a Mac for mobile development?

For iOS development and App Store publishing, effectively yes. Android and cross-platform development targeting Android can be done on Windows or Linux.

### Do I need to know backend development?

You do not have to build servers, but you need to understand HTTP, REST APIs, authentication and data formats. A mobile app almost always talks to a server.
