## Why estimates miss

Deadlines slip not necessarily because the team works badly. Software development means building something that did not exist before, and at the start of a project **the unknowns are at their peak**. An estimate at that point is a forecast, not a promise. The better you understand where errors come from, the calmer you can plan the business around the launch.

## The main causes

- **Incomplete requirements.** "A user account area" can mean very different amounts of work to the client and the developer.
- **Forgotten work.** People estimate visible features but forget testing, deployment, bug fixing, the admin panel, emails and notifications.
- **Integrations.** External services behave differently from their docs, and access arrives late.
- **Changes along the way.** Every "small tweak" adds work, and together they move the date noticeably.
- **Optimism.** People tend to estimate as if everything will go to plan.
- **Waiting for decisions.** A week without an answer from the client is a week added to the timeline.
- **Pressure for a number.** When a team is expected to give a date, it names the desired one, not the realistic one.

## A range instead of a single number

One date creates false precision. A range shows uncertainty honestly.

| Estimate format | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| "Done by March 1" | Nothing about risks or assumptions |
| "8 to 12 weeks" | There is uncertainty, and its limits are known |
| "8–12 weeks if the bank API is available in week one" | A range plus the key assumption |

The earlier the project, the wider the range. As work progresses it should **narrow**. If it is just as wide a month later, find out why.

## How to add a buffer

- Add a reserve **for the whole project**, not for each task: buffers inside individual tasks usually get used up unnoticed.
- Put more reserve on high-uncertainty parts: integrations, new technologies, vague requirements.
- Do not tie hard business events (ad campaigns, public announcements) to the optimistic end of the range.

## How to read a vendor's estimate

Ask a few questions:

- Which **assumptions** is the estimate based on?
- Are testing, deployment and bug fixing included?
- What counts as a finished task?
- Which parts are the riskiest?
- How and when will the estimate be refined?

Be cautious when an estimate is suspiciously precise despite vague requirements, or when questions about assumptions go unanswered.

## What the client can do

- Split the project into **stages with demos** so you see real progress, not percentages in a report.
- Decide what can be cut without losing the point if the date matters more than the scope.
- Answer the team's questions and approve materials quickly.
- Record requirement changes and their effect on the timeline.

## FAQ

### Why not just ask for an exact date?

You can, but the precision will be an illusion: the uncertainty does not go away, it just becomes hidden. A range with assumptions gives you more information for planning.

### How do I know the project is really behind?

Compare working results at demos with the stage plan rather than completion percentages. If stages keep slipping, it is time to discuss scope, priorities or resources.

### Does a fixed price prevent delays?

It shifts the financial risk to the vendor but does not remove uncertainty. The vendor usually builds it into the price or limits the scope more strictly.
